Race on the death certificate and place: the mother's declaration, the family's classification and the certified race of infants across 5,570 Brazilian municipalities, 2013-2023
[Authors, affiliations, ORCID, funding, conflicts of interest and contributions: to be supplied by the authors; not part of the blinded text]
Abstract
Objective: To compare the race recorded on the death certificates of infants in Brazil with the race their mothers declared for themselves and with the race families assign to children, and to find where in the country and over which years they differ and what explains it.
Methods: Cross-sectional study of routinely collected birth and death records and of census microdata. Infant deaths of 2013-2023 were linked one-to-one to their birth records, and the race households report for children in the 2010 Census was set against both. Across 5,570 municipalities, proportions were smoothed by BYM2 models, with Moran's I, a test at state borders and multilevel models.
Results: Among 296,512 linked infant deaths, the certificate recorded the mother's race for 0.76 (95%CI 0.75; 0.78) of children of White mothers, 0.64 (95%CI 0.62; 0.66) of Brown and 0.18 (95%CI 0.17; 0.20) of Black mothers. The share of Brown mothers' infants certified White ranged from 0.09 to 0.76 across municipalities, rose with the municipality's share of White births (composition alone 71% of the deviance) and stepped at state borders. The residual variation lay in the establishment where the death occurred (log-odds variance 0.29 against 0.03 between municipalities). Families classified a Brown couple's child as White in 0.09 in the least White municipalities and 0.14 in the most, a gradient under a quarter of the certificate's, while the birth record recorded mothers whiter than the census where most were White (logit slope 1.13 within regions, 1 if it followed the census).
Conclusion: The race on an infant's death certificate followed the place (establishment, composition, state) more than the family, whose classification of a Brown couple's child changed little with composition. The birth record leans toward the local majority, so against self-declaration the certificate's place dependence is, if anything, understated.
Keywords: Race Factors; Death Certificates; Medical Record Linkage; Spatial Analysis; Infant Mortality; Vital Statistics; Brazil.
Introduction
Mortality by race in Brazil is computed from two classifications made by different people. The numerator is the race on the death certificate. The Ministry of Health instructs that it be asked of the person who informs on the deceased and never decided by the physician from observation [1]; the system does not record who gave the information [2], and in practice the physician or hospital staff often enter it [3]. The denominator is a declaration: the census respondent's, or, on the live birth record since the form of 2011, the mother's declaration of her own race [4]. Rates computed from the two were shown to depend on the source [5,6,7].
Race is not one variable: self-identification and classification by an observer are distinct, though correlated [8]. In a Brazilian national survey, interviewers and respondents agreed on White, Brown or Black in 79% of cases, with agreement varying with the racial composition of the place [9], and Brown people were reclassified as White more often in whiter cities [10]. Parents do not simply transmit their race to their children [11].
What this does to mortality statistics has been measured where a second classification of the same person exists: United States certificates disagreed with survey reports for American Indian decedents, whose death rates were understated [12,13,14], and in São Paulo 17.3% of 1,012 older adults had on the certificate a race other than the one they had reported [15]. A cohort of 19.5 million Brazilian births linked to deaths, classified by the mother's race, found higher mortality before age 5 among children of Indigenous, Black and Brown mothers [16]. To our knowledge no study has compared, for the country, the race on the death certificate with the race on the birth record, or either with the race families assign to their children, and none asked where the two disagree.
Where matters because the composition of the population and the services that fill certificates vary across Brazil's municipalities. Three readings of the certificate make different predictions in space. Under the first, it transmits the mother's race, and the agreement should not depend on place. Under the second, it records what the family would answer about the child, and should follow the family's classification. Under the third, it reflects a classification made by whoever fills it: if that draws on the local population, it should move with the racial composition of the municipality; if it is an administrative practice, it should change at state borders and with the services that certify; if it follows social position, it should follow the wealth and schooling of the place and of the family.
This study aimed to compare the race recorded on the death certificates of infants in Brazil with the race their mothers declared for themselves and with the race families assign to children, and to find where in the country and over which years they differ and what explains it.
Methods
Design and reporting. This is a cross-sectional study of routinely collected birth and death records and of census microdata, reported according to the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statement [17].
Data sources. Death certificates came from the Mortality Information System (SIM) and live birth records from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC), in the public files of the Ministry of Health [18], read in November 2026: deaths of 2013 to 2024 and births of 2000 to 2023. The classification made by families came from the sample of the 2010 Population Census [19] (20,635,472 records representing 190,755,799 people). Municipal boundaries were those of the 2022 mesh [20]; regions, state capitals and metropolitan regions [21,22] were taken from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE). All files are public and carry no names.
The three classifications. The categories are the five of the official classification: branca (White), preta (Black), parda (Brown), amarela (Yellow) and indígena (Indigenous); Yellow is not shown separately because of small numbers. (1) Mother's declaration: the race field of the birth record became the mother's self-declared race with the form introduced in 2011 [4]; it was filled on 2.5% of births in 2010 and 92.7% in 2012 (Supplementary Table S1), so cohorts born from 2012 were taken to carry the mother's race. (2) Family's classification: the 2010 Census asks one resident to report the race of every member of the household and identifies each person's mother when she lives in the household (6,252,654 pair records of children under 25); it concerns other children than those who died, a population reference applied by the mother's race, the child's age and the place. (3) Certificate: the race field of the death certificate (Figure 1).
Linkage of infant deaths. Deaths before one year of age in 2013 to 2023 (383,975 certificates) were linked to birth records by a probabilistic linkage [23] on birth date, sex, municipality of residence of the mother, birth weight and mother's age, keeping one-to-one pairs. 309,203 (80.5%) were linked and 296,512 had the mother's race on the birth record. The estimated share of false links was 0.57-0.75% a year, and on fields not used to link the pairs agreed on delivery type in 98% or more (Supplementary Table S2). The linked share was compared across certified race, size of municipality, region, year and age at death [24,25].
Study populations. (a) Infant deaths of 2013-2023, linked. (b) Children under 10 living with their mother in the 2010 Census sample. Deaths at 1-9 years are in Supplementary Text S1.
Variables of the death and of the birth. Age at death, sex, municipality of residence, year, underlying cause of death, the certifier (attending or substitute physician, medical examiner, death verification service, other), the place and the establishment of death, and the mother's schooling and age and the birth weight.
Variables of the municipality. Racial composition: the share of White mothers among the municipality's live births of 2012-2023. Socioeconomic context [26,27], health services [28], the municipality's own certification practice, quality of recording and place (state capital, metropolitan region, macro-region and state) are defined, with their sources and years, in Supplementary Table S3.
Measures. For each class of mother, the proportion of deaths certified in each race, and the proportion of children the family classified in each race.
Analyses. (1) Smoothing: for each class of mother, the linked infant deaths certified in each race were modelled by municipality as binomial counts with a Besag-York-Mollié effect in the BYM2 parametrisation on the queen contiguity of the 2022 mesh [29,30,31,32]; the census children were modelled the same way. (2) Spatial structure: Moran's I of the empirical-Bayes standardised proportions and the local Moran statistic, 999 permutations [33,34,35]. (3) Composition, state and context: binomial models of the municipal proportions with a spline of the logit share of White births, state effects and the municipal context (GDP per capita, illiteracy, low income, urbanisation, physicians, the shares of deaths certified by a verification service or a medical examiner, blank race), alone and together, with the deviance each block explains. (4) Borders: for pairs of neighbouring municipalities in different states, the difference of their empirical logits regressed on the difference of their states' effects estimated from interior municipalities [36]. (5) Individuals: logistic models of each death (for example, a Brown mother's infant certified White) with a random intercept for the municipality and, crossed, one for the establishment of death, entering composition, context and the death's own variables in sequence [37,38]; and, for 2014-2018, a crossed model with the certifying physician. (6) Time: binomial mixed models of the yearly proportions in 133 intermediate regions with a random slope for the year, and a municipality-year panel splitting each yearly variable into between and within parts [39], with the change between the 2010 and 2022 Censuses as a second time point [26,27]. Details are in Supplementary Text S2.
The family and the birth record against place. Two tests located the gradient with composition. (1) If the Brown category were not the same population everywhere, families in whiter municipalities would report the children of a Brown mother and a Brown father as White more often: in the 2010 Census sample (municipality held) the share of such children aged 0-9 reported White was related to the municipality's share of White births, with the child's age and sex and the mother's schooling held, errors clustered by municipality, and the family's gradient for all Brown mothers' children was split into the partner mix and the rate within couple type. (2) If the birth record bent toward the local majority, its share of White mothers would depart from the census along composition: by municipality, the share of White mothers on the birth records of 2012-2013 was regressed (binomial, on the logit scale) on the share White among the mothers of children aged 0-2 in the 2010 Census sample, and the birth records of 2021-2023 on the women aged 15-49 of the 2022 Census, nationally, within region and within state; a slope of 1 means the birth record follows the census up to a constant (fertility), a slope above 1 that it leans toward the local majority.
Statistical analysis. Proportions from the one-to-one linkage carry 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) from 400 bootstrap resamples of municipalities, because pairs cluster by place; binomial intervals, 3 to 10 times narrower, are in the supplement. For the candidate estimator, the variance of a difference of counts was taken as the sum of the two counts (Poisson), and its proportions exclude municipalities with 25,600 or more live births per year, where it lost precision (Results). Municipalities were grouped by thirds of GDP per capita and as state capitals, other members of metropolitan regions and the interior. Census standard errors are understated. Local Moran statistics are reported at p < 0.05 without correction for multiplicity. No hypothesis test was pre-registered, and the analyses by place and context are exploratory. Analyses used Python and R (R-INLA [40], lme4, esda [41]); the code accompanies the article.
Ethics. The study used only public, anonymised data and, under Resolution 510/2016 of the National Health Council, did not require review by a research ethics committee.
Results
The one-to-one linkage found the birth record of 80.5% of infant deaths; the linked share was 81.1%, 81.1% and 80.2% for deaths certified White, Brown and Black, 61.9% for Indigenous, and was lower after the first month (64.9%) and in the North (76.2%) than in the South (89.0%) (Supplementary Table S2). In municipalities with fewer than 25,600 births per year, the candidate estimator reproduced the linkage's proportions; the deceased was among its candidates for 71.7% of deaths at 1-9 years, against 89.8% of infant deaths (Figure 1, Supplementary Figure S1, Supplementary Tables S4 and S12; Supplementary Text S4).

Three classifications
Families did not classify children as their mothers (Figure 2, Supplementary Table S13). Before one year, the household reported as White 0.88 of the children of White mothers, as Brown 0.71 of the children of Brown mothers and as Black 0.39 of the children of Black mothers; 0.28 of the children of Brown mothers were reported White. The certificate agreed with the family on the exchange between Brown and White, recording White for 0.25 (95%CI 0.23; 0.27) of the infants of Brown mothers, and departed from it elsewhere: it recorded Brown for 0.64 (95%CI 0.62; 0.66) of those of Brown mothers and Black for 0.18 (95%CI 0.17; 0.20) of those of Black mothers, of whom 0.55 (95%CI 0.53; 0.57) were certified Brown (Supplementary Text S4).

The municipality
The proportion of Brown mothers' infants certified White ranged, across the municipalities in which 90% of births occur, from 0.09 to 0.76 (Figure 3A); the proportion of Brown mothers' children aged 0-9 whom families reported White, from 0.10 to 0.36 (Figure 3B). On the logit scale the smoothed municipal values of the certificate varied 4.9 times as much as the family's. The variation was spatially structured (BYM2 mixing parameter 0.99, Moran's I 0.77, 3,459 municipalities; Figure 3D). The certificate classed Brown mothers' infants White more often than families class Brown mothers' children where White births were the majority and less often where they were few (Figure 3C): weighted by births, it exceeded the family by 0.37 in the South and fell short of it by 0.05 in the Northeast. Municipal resolution, local clusters and the other cells are in Supplementary Text S4.

Composition, state and context
For Brown mothers' infants certified White, the municipality's composition alone explained 71% of the deviance between municipalities, the state alone 75%, and the socioeconomic, service and recording context alone 42%; composition and state together explained 81%, and the context added 1% (Figure 4B, Supplementary Table S14). Composition and state each added to the other, and Moran's I of the residuals fell from 0.71 to 0.15 with both (Supplementary Text S4).
At state borders, the difference between two neighbouring municipalities in different states was 0.46 (95%CI 0.26; 0.65) times the difference between their states' effects estimated from municipalities touching no other state (1,069 pairs; Figure 4C); for the certified race differing from the mother's it was 0.35 (95%CI 0.19; 0.52). The certified race changes in a step at state borders, as a state practice would make it, and the step is smaller than the difference between the states' interiors. The split by place within the state and by municipal wealth is in Supplementary Text S4.
In the multilevel models of the 178,656 linked infant deaths of Brown mothers' children, the variance between municipalities of the log-odds of being certified White was 1.32 (median odds ratio 2.99). Composition alone explained 87% of it, the socioeconomic context and place alone 55%, the services and recording alone 43%, and composition with both 89%. The variables of the death and the birth explained -1% without the macro-region: the differences between municipalities are not who dies there or of what. With everything, 91% was explained and the median odds ratio fell to 1.38 (Figure 4D, Supplementary Tables S6 and S14; Supplementary Text S4).
Residual clustering was better captured by the establishment where the death occurred than by the municipality: with a crossed random effect for the establishment (7,429 levels), the variance of the log-odds of Brown mothers' infants being certified White was 0.29 between establishments and 0.03 between municipalities, and for Black mothers' infants certified Black 0.31 and 0.10; the Akaike information criterion fell by 3,411 and 266. Where a municipality has one hospital the two levels cannot be told apart. The certifier and the individual terms of the full model are in Figure 4E and Supplementary Text S4.

Who classifies
The certificate was not a copy of the birth record. Among infants who died in the establishment where they were born, on the day of birth or the next, when the birth record and its mother's race lay at hand, the certificate agreed with the mother's race in 0.67 (95%CI 0.65; 0.68) of deaths, against 0.61 (95%CI 0.60; 0.62) among infants who died elsewhere, and the dependence of Brown mothers' infants certified White on composition was steeper there (slope 0.73 per logit unit, against 0.56 elsewhere). The other cells moved toward the local majority only partly: Brown mothers' infants were certified White as often as not where 58% (95%CI 56%; 60%) of births were White, not where half were (Supplementary Text S4).
Whether the death was investigated, and how, changed the certificate little: agreement with the mother's race was 0.64 (95%CI 0.64; 0.64) not investigated; 0.68 (95%CI 0.67; 0.69) investigated with a home visit or family interview; the other kinds of investigation were alike. Among 82,173 deaths of 2014-2018 with the certifying physician recorded, the log-odds variance of Brown mothers' infants certified White was 0.79 between physicians, 0.46 between establishments and 0.08 between municipalities; with 3.7 deaths per physician that order is not secure.
Time and place
From 2013 to 2023 the certificate moved toward the mother's race for Brown mothers' infants: the White category for them fell by 0.19 (95%CI 0.11; 0.27) in log-odds per decade within intermediate regions (the crude national proportion went from 0.26 to 0.25), and the Brown category rose by 0.35 (Figure 5A, Supplementary Table S7). The change was uneven, by macro-region from -0.55 per decade in the South to -0.05 in the Northeast (Figure 5B; Supplementary Text S4).
Within places, composition moved the certificate far less than it separated places: in the municipality-year panel the within coefficient was about 11% of the between coefficient once corrected for the noise of the yearly share (immediate regions), over the census decade 0.29 against 1.01, and the long panel did not discriminate (Figure 5C-D). Within places, GDP per capita and physicians did not move the certificate; the share of natural deaths certified by a verification service (0.61 (95%CI 0.23; 1.00)) and of deaths with the race left blank (-1.45 (95%CI -1.83; -1.06)) did. Details are in Supplementary Text S3.

The family and the birth record against place
Families carried little of the gradient. The children aged 0-9 of a Brown mother and a Brown father were reported White in 0.09 (95%CI 0.09; 0.09) of households in municipalities with under 10% of White births and in 0.14 (95%CI 0.12; 0.15) where 90% or more were White, most of the rise below 30% (Figure 6A); the logit slope was 0.16 (standard error 0.01; 0.16 with age, sex and schooling held), against 0.66 for the certificate on the same axis. For all Brown mothers the family's share White rose from 0.15 to 0.36: with the partner mix held at the national one it rose to 0.27, with the rate within couple type held to 0.29, about half each; in whiter places Brown mothers more often had White partners (0.14 to 0.39). The birth record leaned toward the local majority. Against the mothers of children aged 0-2 in the 2010 Census sample, the share of White mothers on the birth records of 2012-2013 followed the census with a logit slope of 1.46 nationally, 1.13 within region and 1.12 within state, where 1 means agreement up to a constant (Figure 6B); against the women aged 15-49 of the 2022 Census, the birth records of 2021-2023 gave 1.52, 1.29 and 1.33. In the least White tenth of municipalities 0.04 of mothers were White on the birth record against 0.07 in the census. Sampling error in the census would flatten the slope, not steepen it.

After infancy
After infancy, with an estimator that needs no linkage, Brown mothers' children aged 1-9 were certified White in 0.23 (95%CI 0.16; 0.29) of deaths in municipalities with under 30% of White births and middle GDP per capita and in 0.67 (95%CI 0.54; 0.80) where 60% or more were White (Supplementary Text S1, Tables S9-S11, Figures S5-S6).
Discussion
The race on the death certificate of a child in Brazil was neither the race the mother had declared for herself nor the race families assign to children, and it depended on where the child lived. Across municipalities, the certificate's classification of Brown mothers' infants as White varied 5 times as much (in log-odds variance) as families' classification of Brown mothers' children aged 0-9, in two large contiguous blocks: whiter than the family in the South, Southeast and Centre-West and less White in the North and Northeast. The racial composition of the municipality's births and the state each accounted for about three quarters of that variation and together for four fifths, with a step at state borders; wealth, schooling, urbanisation, the supply of physicians and the services that certify accounted for less than composition and little in addition to it, and the characteristics of the deaths, without the macro-region, accounted for none of it. The White category for Brown mothers' infants became less common over the decade, unevenly between regions.
The three readings set out in the Introduction make different predictions, and the data narrow them (Table 1). The certificate does not transmit the mother's race: its agreement with her varied between regions, and it was only slightly higher where the birth record lay at hand. Nor does it record what the family would answer, as the Ministry of Health instructs [1]: nationally the two agree on the exchange between Brown and White (0.25 on the certificate, 0.28 in the family), but as an average of opposite departures by region, and the certificate used the Black category less than half as often as families (0.18 against 0.39). What remains is a classification by whoever fills the certificate. It moves toward the local majority, partly, in the three cells tested; it has a state component, with a step at state borders; and it varies between the individuals who certify: the variance between physicians (0.79) exceeded that between establishments (0.46) and municipalities (0.08), though with few deaths per physician that order is not secure. A Brown category that is not the same population everywhere could also produce the gradient; families do not show it: a Brown couple's child was reported White only a little more often in whiter places (0.09 to 0.14), under a quarter of the certificate's gradient. The birth record, the reference here, also leaned toward the local majority (logit slope 1.13 within regions against the census), so the certificate's departure from the mothers' own declaration is larger, not smaller, by a selection argument: in whiter places the mothers the birth record still classes as Brown are those it found least ambiguous, so the share of their infants certified White understates what a certifier does to Brown mothers in general there, and the reverse holds in less White places. Three rival readings of a slope above 1 remain: fertility that differs between classes along composition (the comparison of mothers with mothers in 2010 addresses it, and the slope stayed above 1), the census respondent answering for the mother rather than the mother herself, and the two or three years between the 2010 Census and the birth records of 2012-2013. Wealth and services explained little beside composition, though in Brazil they are collinear with it. The within-place change was small and imprecise: when a region's share of White births changed, its certificate moved about 11% as far as the cross-sectional gradient predicts, with an interval that includes no change, and a slow adjustment cannot be excluded.
A classification that follows the local majority is what studies of classification by observers in Brazil describe: agreement between interviewers and respondents varied with the local racial composition [9], and Brown people were reclassified as White more often in whiter cities [10]. The certificate shows the same pattern at the scale of the country: Brown mothers' infants were certified White in 0.13 to 0.79 of linked deaths pooled in the least and the most White of 10 classes of municipalities by their share of White births, a proportion that rose with composition within states and stepped at their borders. One part of the certificate does not follow the local majority as strongly: the Black category, the subject of a companion paper. Its use clustered in space, depended on the state more than on composition, and rose over the decade; whether this follows the growth of Black identification seen between censuses [42,43] cannot be tested here.
Strengths and limitations. The study covers every municipality of the country over eleven years, compares the certificate with the mother's declaration for the same child by one-to-one linkage, and sets the competing explanations against one another rather than adjusting for them. Its limitations are these. (1) The family's classification concerns other children, in 2010, and is a reference by mother's race, age and place. (2) After infancy the estimator finds the deceased only if the family did not move (71.7% at 1-9 years) and cannot describe state capitals. (3) The birth record, the reference, itself leans toward the local majority, and the census comparison is by municipality, not by person. 7.3% of 2012 births were still on the earlier form. (4) Composition is measured from the same mothers' declarations; the census share substituted only in the municipal models. (5) The explanations were compared ecologically, and the certifier and the informant cannot be separated because the certificate does not record who gave the race; the multilevel models used an approximation, with which a Laplace refit agreed. (6) Municipal estimates for the Black category rest on few deaths. (7) Standard errors of the candidate estimator treat counts as Poisson, and census ones ignore the sample design. (8) The analyses by place and context were not pre-specified. (9) Deaths of 2023 may be revised. (10) The family benchmark is not the same child's classification by its own family, and households report children by both parents (a companion paper).
The reference. Two findings of the companion papers weaken the birth record as the reference: it leans toward the local majority, recording mothers whiter than the census in whiter places and less White in less White ones (logit slope 1.13 within regions, 1 if it followed the census), and where hospitals rarely record Black on the admission it counts about half the Black mothers the census holds (5.2% against 10.9%). For this paper, the first means the certificate's dependence on composition, measured against the birth record, is if anything understated against self-declaration; the second means the Black mothers of the less White states are a selected, less ambiguous group, so the certificate's shortfall of the Black category there is if anything understated too.
Implications. An infant's race on the death certificate depends on where the child died, on the state and on the establishment, and only in part on the child's family; statistics by certified race therefore compare places as much as groups. Infant and child deaths that can be linked to their birth records can be classified by the mother's race, as linked cohorts already do [16], which removes the certificate's dependence on place, though not any place-dependence of the birth record itself. Recording on the certificate who informed the race would allow the classification to be audited, and the steps at state borders point to the states' practice as one place to act.
In conclusion, this study set out to compare the race recorded on the death certificates of infants in Brazil with the race their mothers declared for themselves and with the race families assign to children, and to find where in the country and over which years they differ and what explains it. The certificate departed from both the mother's declaration and the family's classification, and followed the place more than the family, whose classification of a Brown couple's child changed little with composition; the birth record leans toward the local majority. The certificate was associated with the racial composition of the municipality and with the state, with a step at state borders, and varied most between establishments and certifiers, more than with the wealth or the services of the place or the circumstances of the death; and it used the Black category for 0.18 of the infants of Black mothers. The design does not establish which classification is correct; it establishes that the certificate departs from both, by amounts that depend on place.
