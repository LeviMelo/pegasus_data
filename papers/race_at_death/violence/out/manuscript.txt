Where the race of young people who die violently changes: the same deaths at birth, in hospital and on the death certificate, Brazil, 2015-2024
[Authors, affiliations, ORCID, funding, conflicts of interest and contributions: to be supplied by the authors; not part of the blinded text]
Abstract
Objective: To measure whether the race recorded for young people in Brazil departs from their race at birth according to how they died, and at which record, in life or at death, the departure arises.
Methods: Cross-sectional study of birth, hospital and death records related without identifiers, Brazil. Deaths at 15-24 years in 2015-2024 of people born in 2000-2009, in municipalities with fewer than 1,600 births a year, were related to the live births and to the earlier hospital admissions with the same birth date, sex and municipality of residence, with records of the other sex as a control for chance agreement. The share recorded White at birth (by staff), at an earlier admission and on the certificate was compared by cause of death, for the same deaths; the primary contrast was the change from birth to the certificate in external causes against natural causes.
Results: Among 87,391 deaths, about half were found among their birth candidates. From birth to the certificate the share White changed by -5 percentage points for natural deaths and by -15 to -16 for the external causes; the difference was -11.5 percentage points (95%CI -14.7; -8.4) for assaults and -10.6 percentage points (95%CI -13.6; -7.6) for the other external causes. For the same deaths with an earlier admission, the share White fell from birth to the hospital record by about 14 points whatever the cause, and from the hospital record to the certificate it changed by +5 points for natural deaths and -6 for assaults (-11.5 percentage points (95%CI -15.6; -7.3)).
Conclusion: In the smaller Brazilian municipalities, young people who died of external causes, assaults no more than the others, were certified White less often than they were recorded at birth. The difference from natural deaths arose after the last hospital record, on the certificate, while the change from birth to hospital was the same for every cause. The large cities, where most homicides occur, require identified linkage.
Keywords: Homicide; Violence; Racial Groups; Death Certificates; Young Adult; Brazil.
Introduction
Black and Brown young people die by homicide far more often than White ones in Brazil [1,2]. These rates take the victim's race from the death certificate, which for violent deaths is often completed by the medical examiner of a medico-legal institute, who has not seen the person alive and records race from the body or from whoever informs it [3]. Race is also a status marker that observers read from circumstances: people incarcerated or poor are seen as darker [4,5], and on United States certificates the race recorded depended on the cause of death [6]. If certifiers record young people killed by firearms as Brown more often than they would the same people dying of other causes, part of the racial gap in homicide is made at death. If instead the young people who are killed were already recorded as Brown before they died, the certificate reports a classification made in life.
The two readings predict different things about a record made before death. Public Brazilian files carry no identifier, but deaths, births and hospital admissions share the birth date, sex and municipality of residence, which in smaller municipalities relate a death to its own birth and admissions with a measurable rate of chance agreement. We found no study that compared the race of the same young people at birth, in hospital and at death by cause. This study aimed to measure whether the race recorded for young people in Brazil departs from their race at birth according to how they died, and at which record, in life or at death, the departure arises.
Methods
Design and data. This is a cross-sectional study of birth, hospital and death records related without identifiers, reported according to the RECORD statement [7]. Deaths came from the Mortality Information System (SIM), births from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC) and admissions from the Hospital Information System (SIH) of the Unified Health System [8].
Population. Deaths at 15-24 years in 2015-2024 of people born in 2000-2009, residents of municipalities with fewer than 1,600 live births a year on average in 2000-2009, where a birth date, sex and municipality are shared by few other people. Causes (underlying cause, ICD-10): natural (A00-R99); transport accidents (V01-V99); suicide (X60-X84); assault by firearm (X93-X95); assault by other means (X85-Y09 otherwise); legal intervention (Y35-Y36); events of undetermined intent (Y10-Y34); other external causes, including injury codes (S, T) filed as underlying causes. Municipalities are those with fewer than 1,600 births a year in 2000-2009 for every comparison except the strata of Figure 3, which come from a tabulation that classes them by their births of 2012-2023.
Race at birth and certified race. For each death, the live births of 2000-2009 with the same birth date, sex and municipality of residence are its candidates; the deceased is among them unless the family moved. The births of the other sex on the same day and in the same municipality cannot contain the deceased and estimate the chance candidates; their count, scaled by the ratio of births of the two sexes, was subtracted from the candidates by race at birth and certified race, which estimates the cross-classification of the deaths found (Supplementary Text S1; validated against one-to-one linkage in infants, where it holds below 25,600 births a year). Before 2011 the birth record carried the newborn's colour as recorded by staff, not the mother's declaration. Proportions are of deaths recorded in a race at birth.
Race at an earlier admission. The same design related each death to the SIH admissions of 2010-2024 of people born in 2000-2009 with its birth date, sex and municipality that ended at least 30 days before the death and not in death, with the admissions of the other sex, scaled by the ratio of the two sexes' admissions in the municipality and birth year, as the control. The hospital's race (White, Black, Brown) was cross-classified with the certified race; intervals came from a bootstrap over municipalities (Figure 1).

Analysis. Cross-classifications were compared between causes, within strata of the certifier (attending physician, medical examiner, death verification service), the place of death, the mother's schooling at birth and the municipality's composition, and standardised to the composition of natural deaths. The primary contrast, stated before the hospital records were analysed, is the change of the share White from birth to the certificate in external causes against natural causes. Birth, hospital and certificate were compared for the same deaths by multiplying, death by death, the estimate of each race at birth and of each race in hospital (the chance parts of the two are independent). Intervals come from bootstrap replicates over municipalities (500 or 1,000); those of the strata in Figure 3 are of a difference of counts and omit the variance of the denominator. Analyses used Python 3.11; the code accompanies the article. Ethics: public, anonymised data; under Resolution 510/2016 of the National Health Council no review was required.
Results
Of 87,391 deaths at 15-24 years in the smaller municipalities, 25,869 were of natural causes, 22,960 assaults by firearm and 1,314 legal interventions. The deceased was found among the candidate births in an estimated 43%-52% of deaths of the larger cause groups, alike across causes; the others had moved or were born elsewhere.
From birth to the certificate
Among the deaths found, the share White was 0.50 at birth and 0.45 on the certificate for natural deaths, a change of -4.9 percentage points (95%CI -7.1; -2.6) (Table 1, Figure 2A). Every external cause lost more: transport accidents 0.54 and 0.38; suicide 0.57 and 0.42; other external causes 0.45 and 0.30; assault by firearm 0.34 and 0.18; assault by other means 0.39 and 0.24. Against natural deaths, the change was -11.5 percentage points (95%CI -14.7; -8.4) for assaults and -10.6 percentage points (95%CI -13.6; -7.6) for the other external causes together: assaults lost no more than transport accidents or suicides. Of those White at birth, 0.69 (95%CI 0.66; 0.72) were certified White after a natural death and 0.37 (95%CI 0.33; 0.41) after a firearm assault; of those Brown at birth, 0.68 (95%CI 0.65; 0.70) and 0.84 (95%CI 0.82; 0.86) were certified Brown, and 0.22 (95%CI 0.20; 0.25) and 0.08 (95%CI 0.06; 0.09) White (Figure 2B).

Among young people White at birth, those who died by assault were certified White less often than those who died of natural causes in every fifth of municipal composition (from 0.17 against 0.39 where under 20% of births were White to 0.79 against 0.91 where 80% or more were, with overlapping intervals there), and standardised to the composition of natural deaths (0.45 against 0.65); by region and period the difference kept its direction (Supplementary Table S1). It was present whoever certified: 0.37 (95%CI 0.34; 0.41) of assaults certified by medical examiners and 0.40 (95%CI 0.24; 0.56) by attending physicians, against 0.60 (95%CI 0.57; 0.63) of other external deaths certified by medical examiners. The share certified White rose with the mother's schooling at birth for every cause: among those White at birth who died by assault, 0.26 (95%CI 0.21; 0.30) with 0-3 years of schooling and 0.52 (95%CI 0.41; 0.63) with 8-11 (Figure 3).

Birth, hospital and certificate for the same deaths
An earlier admission, ending at least 30 days before the death and not in death, was found for 47% of young people who died of natural causes and 21% of those who died by firearm; each person was counted once, and 29% and 37% of these persons had no race among White, Black and Brown on their admissions. For the same deaths at birth, in hospital and on the certificate (Figure 4B), the share White fell from birth to the hospital record by 14 points for natural deaths, 17 for transport accidents and 15 for firearm assaults (assaults against natural +0.1 percentage points (95%CI -4.8; +5.1)). From the hospital record to the certificate it changed by +5 points for natural deaths, -0 for transport accidents and -6 for assaults (assaults against natural -11.5 percentage points (95%CI -15.6; -7.3)). The two records exchanged categories asymmetrically by cause: of young people the hospital recorded Brown, 0.30 (95%CI 0.28; 0.32) were certified White after a natural death and 0.13 (95%CI 0.11; 0.15) after a firearm assault, while of those the hospital recorded White, 0.74 (95%CI 0.72; 0.76) and 0.46 (95%CI 0.40; 0.51) were certified White (Figure 4A, Supplementary Table S2). In the admission in which the young person died, the hospital recorded White 0.36 of natural deaths and the certificate 0.45; for firearm deaths 0.22 and 0.26: the hospital's record was darker than the certificate for every cause. The results were alike for men alone and with admissions ending at least 180 days before death (0.46 of those recorded White certified White after a firearm death). With the admissions' birth dates shifted by 7 or 14 days either way, where the person cannot be among them, the estimator still counted on average 12% as many persons as found for firearm deaths and -4% for natural deaths, a residual of chance agreement the control did not remove, which draws the hospital shares of violent deaths toward their municipalities' population.

Discussion
Young people who died of external causes in the smaller Brazilian municipalities were certified White less often than they had been recorded at birth, by about three times as much as those who died of natural causes, and assaults no more than the other external causes. For the same deaths, the change from birth to the hospital record was alike for every cause; the difference between causes arose afterwards, between the last hospital record and the certificate, where natural deaths regained part of the White category and violent deaths did not.
Three readings were set against each other. If the young people who die violently were reclassified in life, the hospital record would already be darker for them than for others, relative to birth; it was not: every cause lost about the same share White between birth and hospital, and the lower hospital share of victims of assault restates their lower share at birth. If the classification were made at death, the certificate would depart from the last record of life by cause, as on United States certificates by cause of death [6]; it did, by about 11 points between assaults and natural deaths. A third reading is that hospital clerks code darker than other observers; the hospital record was darker than the birth record for every cause, and darker than the certificate in the admission of the death itself, so the hospital is not a neutral reference of life, and the comparison between causes, not the level, carries the inference. Race read as a status marker changes with circumstances [4,5]; here it changed between records made by different observers, by an amount that depended on the observer more than on the cause, except at death.
Who certified did not explain the difference: assaults were certified White less often among those White at birth whether a medical examiner or an attending physician signed. The mother's schooling at birth ordered the certified race for every cause.
Strengths and limitations. The study follows the same deaths through records made at birth, in life and at death, counts each person once, measures chance agreement with a negative control and shifted-date placebos, and compares the causes on one population. Limitations: it covers the smaller municipalities, where a birth date, sex and municipality are shared by few people; the capitals and large cities, where most homicides occur, cannot be reached without identified records, which require a data-use agreement; people who moved are not found, about half of the deaths, alike for every cause; only about a fifth of those who died by violence had an earlier admission, a selected group, and about a third of the persons found carried no race on their admissions; the race at birth is the newborn's colour recorded by staff; the placebo leaves a residual of chance agreement of about a tenth of the persons found for violent deaths, which pulls their hospital shares toward the municipality's population; and the strata of Figure 3 come from a tabulation with a different set of municipalities and intervals that omit the variance of their denominator.
Implications. Rates of homicide by race in Brazil take the victim's race from a certificate that, in these municipalities, records victims as White less often than their birth records did, by more than for natural deaths; part of that difference is made at death. Whether this holds in the large cities requires identified linkage.
In conclusion, this study set out to measure whether the race recorded for young people in Brazil departs from their race at birth according to how they died, and at which record, in life or at death, the departure arises. Young people who died of external causes were certified White less often than at birth, by more than those who died of natural causes; the change from birth to the hospital record was the same for every cause, and the difference by cause arose after it, on the certificate.
