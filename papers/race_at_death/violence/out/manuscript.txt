Recorded darker before they were killed: the race of young people at birth, in hospital during life and on the death certificate, by cause of death, Brazil, 2015-2024
[Authors, affiliations, ORCID, funding, conflicts of interest and contributions: to be supplied by the authors; not part of the blinded text]
Abstract
Objective: To measure whether the race recorded for young people in Brazil departs from their race at birth according to how they died, and whether the departure is already present in hospital records made during life or arises on the death certificate.
Methods: Cross-sectional study of birth, hospital and death records related without identifiers, Brazil. Deaths at 15-24 years in 2015-2024 of people born in 2000-2009 were related to the live births and to the earlier hospital admissions with the same birth date, sex and municipality of residence, with records of the other sex as a control for chance agreement, in municipalities with fewer than 1,600 births a year. The race recorded at birth (by staff, before the 2011 form), at an earlier admission (one record per person) and at the admission in which the person died was set against the certified race, by cause of death.
Results: Among deaths found among their birth candidates, the share White fell from birth to the certificate by 5 percentage points for natural deaths and by 16-17 for transport, suicide and firearm deaths. Young people who died by assault were already recorded White less often by an earlier admission (0.24, against 0.38 for natural deaths). From that record to the certificate the share White changed by -0.03 for assaults, -0.03 for transport and +0.08 for natural deaths; for assaults the certificate departed from the hospital record more often in both directions (recorded White and certified White: 0.46 for firearm deaths, 0.74 for natural deaths).
Conclusion: In the smaller Brazilian municipalities, young people who die of external causes, and most of all by assault, were recorded darker than at birth already during life; the certificate after a violent death is less consistent with earlier records but, net, about as dark as them. The large cities, where most homicides occur, require identified linkage.
Keywords: Homicide; Violence; Racial Groups; Death Certificates; Young Adult; Brazil.
Introduction
Black and Brown young people die by homicide far more often than White ones in Brazil [1,2]. These rates take the victim's race from the death certificate, completed for violent deaths by the medical examiner of a medico-legal institute, who in most cases has never seen the person alive [3]. Race is also a status marker that observers read from circumstances: people incarcerated or poor are seen as darker [4,5], and on United States certificates the race recorded depended on the cause of death [6]. If certifiers record young people killed by firearms as Brown more often than they would the same people dying of other causes, part of the racial gap in homicide is made at death. If instead the young people who are killed were already recorded as Brown before they died, the certificate reports a classification made in life.
The two readings predict different things about a record made before death. Public Brazilian files carry no identifier, but deaths, births and hospital admissions share the birth date, sex and municipality of residence, which in smaller municipalities relate a death to its own birth and admissions with a measurable rate of chance agreement. We know of no study that compared the race of the same young people at birth, in hospital and at death by cause. This study aimed to measure whether the race recorded for young people in Brazil departs from their race at birth according to how they died, and whether the departure is already present in hospital records made during life or arises on the death certificate.
Methods
Design and data. This is a cross-sectional study of birth, hospital and death records related without identifiers, reported according to the RECORD statement [7]. Deaths came from the Mortality Information System (SIM), births from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC) and admissions from the Hospital Information System (SIH) of the Unified Health System [8].
Population. Deaths at 15-24 years in 2015-2024 of people born in 2000-2009, residents of municipalities with fewer than 1,600 live births a year on average in 2000-2009, where a birth date, sex and municipality are shared by few other people. Causes (underlying cause, ICD-10): natural (A00-R99); transport accidents (V01-V99); suicide (X60-X84); assault by firearm (X93-X95); assault by other means (X85-Y09 otherwise); legal intervention (Y35-Y36); other external causes.
Race at birth and certified race. For each death, the live births of 2000-2009 with the same birth date, sex and municipality of residence are its candidates; the deceased is among them unless the family moved. The births of the other sex on the same day and in the same municipality cannot contain the deceased and estimate the chance candidates; their count, scaled by the ratio of births of the two sexes, was subtracted from the candidates by race at birth and certified race, which estimates the cross-classification of the deaths found (Supplementary Text S1; validated against one-to-one linkage in infants, where it holds below 25,600 births a year). Before 2011 the birth record carried the newborn's colour as recorded by staff, not the mother's declaration. Proportions are of deaths recorded in a race at birth.
Race at an earlier admission. The same design related each death to the SIH admissions of 2010-2024 of people born in 2000-2009 with its birth date, sex and municipality that ended at least 30 days before the death and not in death, with the admissions of the other sex, scaled by the ratio of the two sexes' admissions in the municipality and birth year, as the control. The hospital's race (White, Black, Brown) was cross-classified with the certified race; intervals came from a bootstrap over municipalities.
Analysis. Cross-classifications were compared between causes, within strata of the certifier (attending physician, medical examiner, death verification service), the place of death, the mother's schooling at birth and the municipality's composition, and standardised to the composition of natural deaths. Standard errors of the candidate estimates are those of a difference of counts (Poisson); they omit the variance of the denominator. Analyses used Python 3.11; the code accompanies the article. Ethics: public, anonymised data; under Resolution 510/2016 of the National Health Council no review was required.
Results
Of 80,326 deaths at 15-24 years in the smaller municipalities, 23,780 were of natural causes, 20,795 assaults by firearm and 1,268 legal interventions. The deceased was among the candidate births in 47%-54% of deaths, alike for every cause; the others had moved or were born elsewhere.
From birth to the certificate
Of young people recorded White at birth, 0.68 (95%CI 0.64; 0.71) were certified White when they died of natural causes, against 0.58 (95%CI 0.54; 0.61) in transport accidents, 0.62 (95%CI 0.55; 0.69) in suicides, 0.35 (95%CI 0.31; 0.39) in assaults by firearm and 0.32 (95%CI 0.15; 0.49) in legal interventions (Table 1, Figure 1). Because fewer of those who died by assault were White at birth, the margins show the net change: among the deaths found, the share White was 0.48 at birth and 0.43 on the certificate for natural deaths, 0.53 and 0.37 for transport accidents, 0.53 and 0.38 for suicides, and 0.33 and 0.16 for firearm assaults: every external cause lost more White between birth and death than natural causes did, assaults no more than transport accidents.

The lower share certified White among those White at birth who died by assault did not come from where they lived: it held in every fifth of municipal composition (from 0.17 against 0.39 for natural deaths where under 20% of births were White to 0.79 against 0.91 where 80% or more were), standardised to the composition of natural deaths (0.45 against 0.65), in each region and in both halves of the decade (Supplementary Table S1). It held whoever certified: 0.37 (95%CI 0.34; 0.41) of assaults certified by medical examiners and 0.40 (95%CI 0.24; 0.56) by attending physicians, against 0.60 (95%CI 0.57; 0.63) of other external deaths certified by medical examiners. It fell steeply with the mother's schooling at birth: among those White at birth who died by assault, 0.26 (95%CI 0.21; 0.30) were certified White when the mother had 0-3 years of schooling and 0.52 (95%CI 0.41; 0.63) with 8-11 (Figure 2).

Hospital records made during life
An earlier admission, ending at least 30 days before the death, was found for 47% of young people who died of natural causes and 21% of those who died by firearm, with 2.0 and 0.2 admissions per death; each person was counted once. Those who later died by assault had been recorded White less often in hospital (0.24, against 0.38 for natural and 0.40 for other external deaths). From the hospital record to the certificate, the share White changed by +0.08 for natural deaths, -0.03 for transport accidents and -0.03 for firearm assaults (Figure 3). The two records agreed less for violent deaths, in both directions: of those recorded White in hospital, 0.74 (95%CI 0.72; 0.76) were certified White after a natural death, 0.70 (95%CI 0.66; 0.75) after a transport accident and 0.46 (95%CI 0.40; 0.51) after a firearm assault, while of those recorded Brown, 0.13 (95%CI 0.11; 0.15) of firearm deaths were certified White (Supplementary Table S2). The results were the same for men alone (0.46 for firearm deaths) and with admissions ending at least 180 days before death (0.46). In the admission in which the young person died, the hospital recorded White 0.22 of firearm deaths and the certificate 0.26; of those the hospital recorded White, 0.65 were certified White, against 0.83 of natural deaths. With the admissions' birth dates moved by a week, where the person cannot be among them, the estimator still counted 12% as many persons for firearm deaths and 3% for natural deaths, a residual of chance agreement the control did not remove.

Discussion
Young people who died of external causes in the smaller Brazilian municipalities were recorded White less often on the death certificate than at birth, by about three times as much as those who died of natural causes, and assaults no more than transport accidents or suicides. Hospital records made during their lives already showed most of that change for those who later died by assault: they had been recorded White less often than those who died of other causes. From the hospital record to the certificate the net change was small for every external cause; what distinguished violent deaths was that the two records agreed less, in both directions.
Two readings were set against each other. Labelling at death would make the certificate darker than any record made in life, for violent deaths above all, as found on United States certificates by cause of death [6]. Selection or reclassification in life would make the people who die violently already darker in the records of their lives. The data favour the second for the net change: those who were killed had been recorded White less often in hospital, and the certificate, net, was about as dark as that record. The first survives in a weaker form: certificates of violent deaths departed from the hospital's record more often, in both directions, which is what a classification made from a body and the circumstances of death, with no family to ask, would produce. Race read as a status marker changes over a life [4,5]; here the change is visible years before death.
The mother's schooling at birth ordered the certified race for every cause, and for assaults more steeply: young people of less schooled mothers were certified darker. Social position at birth, the circumstances of a life and the manner of death all move the record, and the design separates only the last from the others.
Strengths and limitations. The study follows the same young people through records made by different observers at birth, in life and at death, counts each person once, and measures chance agreement with a negative control and a shifted-date placebo. Limitations: it covers the smaller municipalities, where a birth date, sex and municipality are shared by few people; the capitals and large cities, where most homicides occur, cannot be reached without identified records, which require a data-use agreement (the Center for Data and Knowledge Integration for Health's 100 Million Cohort, or a state health secretariat); people who moved are not found, about half of the deaths, alike for every cause; only a fifth of those who died by violence had an earlier admission, a selected group; the birth, hospital and certificate shares are compared on overlapping, not identical, sets of deaths; the race at birth is the newborn's colour recorded by staff; the placebo shows a residual of chance agreement of about a tenth of the estimate for violent deaths; and the standard errors of the birth estimates omit the variance of their denominator.
Implications. Rates of homicide by race in Brazil take the victim's race from a certificate that, in these municipalities, classes victims about as hospital staff did during their lives and darker than at birth. The racial gap in violent death is not mainly made at death; that it is so for the large cities requires identified linkage.
In conclusion, this study set out to measure whether the race recorded for young people in Brazil departs from their race at birth according to how they died, and whether the departure is already present in hospital records made during life or arises on the death certificate. Young people who died violently were recorded darker than at birth, and the change was already present in hospital records made during their lives; the certificate after a violent death departed from those records more often but, net, not darker.
