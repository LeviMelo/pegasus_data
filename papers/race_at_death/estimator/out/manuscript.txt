Cross-classifying two registers without linking them: candidates less a negative control from birth date, sex and municipality, tested on Brazilian birth, death and hospital records
[Authors, affiliations, ORCID, funding, conflicts of interest and contributions: to be supplied by the authors; not part of the blinded text]
Abstract
Objective: To state, validate and give the variance of an estimator of the cross-classification of a variable recorded in two registers that share no identifier, only the birth date, sex and municipality of residence.
Methods: For each record of one register (a death), the records of the other (births) sharing its birth date, sex and municipality are counted by class; the other sex's records of the same day estimate the chance agreements and are subtracted. The estimator was run on the units a one-to-one linkage paired, for 383,975 Brazilian infant deaths (2013-2023) and 3,766,913 hospital admissions that ended in death (2016-2022), with a shifted-date placebo and a simulation of its bias and coverage.
Results: On the linked infants of municipalities under 25,600 births a year (85% of births), the estimate differed from the linkage by 0.004 on average over 20 cells (without the control, 0.071). On admissions, against its target (the people the key finds), it differed by 0.001 with 3 of 20 cells outside the interval, and in every age band by 0.001-0.004. The shifted-date placebo found almost no one. In simulation the estimator was nearly unbiased for the people found where its assumptions held. Where it could be measured (admissions linked to certificates), findability fell from 91% for White to 81% for Indigenous.
Conclusion: A cross-classification between registers can be estimated without linking anyone, for the people whose key the two registers share, with an interval that held against that target; its bias is the selection of who shares the key.
Keywords: Medical Record Linkage; Vital Statistics; Data Accuracy; Bias; Negative Controls; Ecological Inference; Simulation; Brazil.
Introduction
Many questions in epidemiology need the cross-classification of a variable recorded twice for the same people in two registers: the race declared at birth against the race certified at death, the diagnosis of an admission against the underlying cause on the certificate. Linking the records gives it, and computers have linked vital records since [1], formalised as probabilistic linkage [2]. Where the registers carry no identifier, linkage rests on partially identifying variables, its errors bias what is estimated from the linked pairs [3,4], the rate of false matches must itself be estimated [5], and methods correct regression on linked data for known or modelled probabilities of a false link [6,7], correct cross-tabulations of linked files by a bootstrap of the linkage [8], carry every candidate record into the analysis by multiple imputation [9], or model the linkage and the analysis jointly [10]. These linkage-error methods work from individual pairs. A cohort of 114 million Brazilians has been linked to national health databases with 93-97% true matches [11], outside the public files, which carry no identifier.
Another tradition estimates a cross-classification from aggregates. Ecological regression infers individual behaviour from the counts of areas [12]; the aggregate data alone do not identify the two unobserved fractions of a table, and the assumptions that identify them have to be stated and examined [13]; the R x C case has frequentist and Bayesian solutions [14]. The estimator studied here is close to that tradition: a cross-moment of the counts of two registers over key cells (birth date, sex, municipality), with a negative-control regressor, the other sex's births, that removes the chance agreements the cells themselves create. It differs in that a person is in the key cell, which makes each cell's expectation linear in the table sought, and it is compared below with a Goodman regression on the same cells.
After the first year of life a Brazilian death certificate shares with the person's birth record only the birth date, the sex and the municipality of residence: the mother's fields and the birth weight, which allow infant deaths to be linked one to one, are not filled. Where many children are born a day, the key is shared by several births and no individual link exists. Yet the question needs only the table, not who is whom. If the false agreements could be counted, they could be subtracted from the table of all agreements. The idea is borrowed from negative controls, comparisons known to carry a bias and not the effect [15], which empirical calibration also uses to correct intervals [16,17]: here, records that agree with a death as often by chance as its candidates do and can never be the person. The births of the other sex on the same day in the same municipality are such records.
This study aimed to state, validate and give the variance of an estimator of the cross-classification of a variable recorded in two registers that share no identifier, only the birth date, sex and municipality of residence: its assumptions and estimands, a variance that includes the denominator of each proportion and the covariances of deaths sharing a key, tests against linked records with known answers on exactly the units the linkage paired, a placebo, comparisons with simpler estimators, and a simulation study of its bias and coverage.
Methods
Design and data. This is a methodological study with validation against linked records and a simulation study, reported according to the RECORD statement [18] and, for the simulation, the ADEMP structure [19]. Live births came from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC), deaths from the Mortality Information System (SIM) and admissions that ended in death from the Hospital Information System of the Unified Health System (SIH), all public files of the Ministry of Health [20]. The race on a birth record is the mother's declaration of her own race since the 2011 form; on a certificate it is recorded by the certifier or hospital staff. Terms. The key is the birth date, sex and municipality of residence. A unit is a death (or an admission that ended in death). A unit is linked when the stored one-to-one linkage paired it with a record of the other register; findable when, besides, that record shares its key; found is the estimator's share T(+, +) over the units, the people the key finds in one of the four classes of the rows. A unit's row is the class on the other register's record (the mother's race for births), its column the class recorded for the unit.
The estimator. Let a group G hold units i with key x_i and class k_i in the first register, and let the second register hold records r with key x_r and class j_r. The candidates of i in class j are C_i(j), the records with x_r = x_i and j_r = j. Its control K_i(j) is the number of records of class j with the birth date and municipality of i and the other sex, times s_i, the ratio of the register's records of i's sex to those of the other sex in that municipality and birth year. The estimator is T(j, k) = sum over the units of G certified k of C_i(j) - K_i(j) (Figure 1). Three assumptions are used. (A1) The person's own record is among the candidates with a probability t_j that may depend on the class j but not, given j, on the class k: the person kept the key (did not move, and the birth date and municipality were recorded alike). (A2) Given the day and the municipality, the records of the two sexes have the same composition by class and stand in the ratio s, and the day's excess variation is shared by the two sexes: the chance candidates' expectation, also for the days on which a death occurred, is the control's. (A3) A record related to the person and sharing the key is of the person's class: a twin of the same sex adds to the candidates, one of the other sex to the control, both in the person's own cell. Under A2 and A3 the expectation of T(j, k) is t_j N(j, k) plus a twins' term in the person's own cell, with N(j, k) the units of G by (j, k). The row T(j, k) / T(j, +) estimates P(k | j) under A1-A3; as a ratio of sums it is consistent, with no finite moments where the row sum can be non-positive. The row sums over T(+, +) estimate the declared mix, and a rate by declared class (row sum over person-years by that class) gives ratios between classes, both only if t_j is the same for all j. No individual is linked, and the case of one candidate (the unique link) is included. A unit whose birth date or municipality differs between the registers is not among its candidates; it adds chance only, which its control removes.

Variance. Five standard errors were compared (Supplementary Text S1), each by the delta method with a proportion's row sum as denominator. (i) Counts, the earlier practice: candidates plus control over the row's sum as if it were known. (ii) Model: with the register's records Poisson, a cell is the people found plus the chance candidates less the control, the control's variance carrying its scale squared, units that share a key and a class counted together. (iii) Exact model: as (ii) with the covariances of the cells of a row, since deaths that share a key cell share their records whatever their class, and of the two sexes, since a day's girls are the candidates of girls' deaths and the control of boys'. (iv) Chance: the error in the table of these units only, in which the people found are fixed. (v) Clusters: Taylor linearisation with municipalities as sampled units, which allows for dependence within a municipality and puts the variation of the classification between municipalities in the error.
Validation 1: infant deaths and their births. Infant deaths of 2013-2023 (383,975) were linked to their birth records by probabilistic linkage [2] on birth date, sex, residence, birth weight and the mother's age; 80.5% were linked, with an estimated 0.57-0.75% of false links a year. The estimator was run on the units themselves, counted by birth date, sex and residence alone: on the linked units (the table to be reproduced is theirs), on all units, on the unlinked units, and on the findable ones, by the municipality's mean births a year. The twenty cells (four races on the birth record by four races and none on the certificate) were compared with the linked pairs'. As a baseline, the candidates were also tabulated without the control.
Validation 2: admissions that ended in death and their certificates. The 3,766,913 admissions of 2016-2022 that ended in death in the year of the admission's end were the units, the death certificates of the same year the register, the race recorded by the hospital and the race on the certificate the two classes. The known answer was a probabilistic linkage of the two on birth date, sex, the day of death and the hospital or its municipality, 0.66-1.00% of false links a year, 95.9% of the units linked. The estimator used birth date, sex and residence, and the year, and the control was scaled within birth year and municipality. This pair differs from the first in the register (deaths of all ages instead of births), in who assigns the classes (hospital staff and certifiers), and in the density of chance agreements, which is lower; its known answer also tells which linked pairs share the key. Size classes were the municipality's mean certificates a year.
Tests of the assumptions on the real units. Placebo: the key birth date of every unit was shifted by 7 or 14 days, forward or back (the same weekday), so that no unit has its person among its candidates; every T(j, k) should be zero within its error, and the z-scores give an empirical null [16,17]. Sex: the table was estimated for boys and for girls apart (control from the other sex) and compared cell by cell. Scale: the control was scaled within the municipality, the state or the nation. Findability (A1): among linked units, the share findable by the class on the other register, by that class and the unit's, and by age. Comparators, on all units and the same cells: the unique-key link (units with one candidate only), uniform attribution (each of c candidates takes 1/c), and Goodman's regression by least squares of the deaths of each class on the births of each class over the key cells without intercept [12], its coefficients normalised by row.
Simulation (ADEMP). Aims: bias and coverage of the estimator, and of its errors, under departures from A1-A3. Data-generating mechanism: the 2018 birth cohort of every municipality by declared race, spread over days with a weekday and seasonal pattern (stronger on weekdays for White births); deaths by declared race at the rates estimated at 1-9 years, 77,693 in all as in 2013-2024, drawn from one cohort; the certified race drawn from the infant linkage's table for the decile of White births of the municipality where the death is certified (Supplementary Text S2). Scenarios: ideal (no departure; all deaths of the twelve cohorts in one cohort, so that deaths sharing a day are twelve times as frequent as in the data); twins (2% of births twins, dying at twice the rate); movers (28% of children dying resident in another municipality of the state, certified there); unequal (movers 22% of White and 30-34% of other children); selective (movers 22%, and 12 points more among the children certified in a class other than the declared one); sparse (as ideal, with a twelfth of the deaths); a2 (boys 52.5% of White and 50.5% of Black and Indigenous births, breaking A2); and, added after the second review, overdisp (a day's births overdispersed, partly shared by the sexes, at the dispersion measured in SINASC 2018), keyerr and keyerr_k (a wrong birth date on the death record at the rate by class that the admissions' findability implies, in the second also more often when the class recorded differs from the declared), pooled12 (twelve independent sparse cohorts pooled, the real density of deaths per key and the real number of deaths), munfx (a municipality effect on the odds of being certified the declared class, of the size measured in the linked infants) and application (movers, twins, pooled cohorts, overdispersion, municipality effects and wrong dates together). Estimands: P(k | j) for all deaths of a group of municipalities by size (the target) and for those found among their candidates; the declared mix; the White, Black and Indigenous to Brown rate ratios, truth by deaths by municipality of birth over births of the same municipalities. Methods: the estimator and its errors from the package function and the exact model error. Performance: bias with its Monte Carlo error, median bias, empirical standard error, mean and median standard error, variance ratio, coverage of the 95% interval with its Monte Carlo error, and the share of replicates with a non-positive row sum (failed), with the moments among the valid ones.
Unequal shares found. Where t differs between classes the declared mix and rate ratios are biased by the ratio of their t: a ratio between classes a and b estimates the true ratio times t_a / t_b. The t_j were measured where linked data allow (Results) and enter the simulation; the size of the bias is shown by it, as a bias analysis [21]. The limit of 25,600 births a year was chosen on the infant validation itself. Ethics: public, anonymised data; under Resolution 510/2016 of the National Health Council no review was required. Analysis code: the package pegasus_data (functions candidate_counts, pair_candidate_counts, candidate_estimates) and the scripts of the paper's repository.
Results
Infant deaths. On exactly the units the linkage paired, below 25,600 births a year the estimator reproduced the linked table with a mean absolute difference over the twenty cells of 0.002 in municipalities under 400 births a year, 0.021 at 6,400-25,599 and 0.004 over all of them, the largest 0.02 (Table 1, Figure 2A); the candidates without the control differed by 0.071 on average. For calibration, 0 of 20 cells lay outside the 95% model interval, 0 outside the exact model interval and 1 outside the chance interval, with a standard deviation of the z-scores of 0.72, 0.73 and 0.78: for infants the intervals were adequate or conservative, because the sampling error is large and the key's own errors small (the linked units share the key in 95% of cases). Run on all units, the unlinked ones included, the difference from the linked table was 0.004; the estimator found 93% of the linked and 77% of the unlinked infants, and the two tables differed by 0.019 on average over the cells with 50 or more units (20 cells; z-score sd 2.00). In the 7 municipalities of 25,600 births or more the chance candidates overwhelmed the people: the largest difference was 0.72. Infants declared Black on the birth record were certified Black in 0.141 (95%CI 0.127; 0.155) by the estimator and 0.141 in the linked pairs, in the smallest municipalities.

Who shares the key. A1 is testable where linked data exist, and it fails. Among admissions linked to a certificate, the share whose certificate shares birth date, sex and residence was 91.3% for certified White, 89.6% Black, 88.2% Brown and 80.7% Indigenous; within the White row 92.9% where the hospital recorded White, 88.8% Brown and 88.4% no race. Findability depends on the class, and given the class on the other register's class, so t_j is not constant and A1 does not hold: a rate ratio of Indigenous to Brown children estimated through the key is scaled by 0.92 before any moving. Among linked infants the shares were 95.5%, 95.3%, 94.7% and 93.5%. The differences between the units that share the key and those that do not are in part differences in recording: of the linked units certified White, the hospital's race was missing in 17.6% of the findable and 24.2% of the unfindable, and the share recorded White among those with a recorded race was 76.2% against 66.2%, a gap of 10.0 percentage points (12.6 before conditioning on a recorded race). Of linked units, 77% had a hospital race in one of the five classes and 97% a race on the certificate. The unfindable are thus partly the units with sloppier records, not necessarily other people.
Tests of the control on the real units. With the key date shifted by one or two weeks (the same weekday) the estimator found 0.3% or less of the infant units and 0.2% or less of the admissions (against 93% and 83% unshifted); the z-scores of its cells had a mean absolute value of at most 0.21 and a standard deviation of 0.77-1.15 for infants and 1.07-1.58 for admissions (Supplementary Table S3). Boys and girls estimated apart gave tables that differed by 0.009 on average for infants (20 cells, z-score sd 0.85) and 0.007 for admissions (20 cells, 4.23, 60% outside 1.96): for infants the sexes agreed within their errors, as A2 predicts, but for a register of deaths of all ages they did not, as the sexes' different composition by age and race implies (Discussion). Scaling the control within the state or the nation instead of the municipality changed the mean difference from the linked table by at most 0.0004 for infants.
Admissions that ended in death. Chance candidates were fewer than in births: 0.02 a unit where a municipality recorded fewer than 100 certificates a year and 0.91 at 6,400 or more (Supplementary Table S6). On the linked units the estimator differed from their table by 0.0057 on average and 0.016 at most over the twenty cells (Figure 2B). Where chance candidates were few the control changed little; at 6,400 certificates a year or more the candidates without it differed by 0.037 on average, with it by 0.009. Run on all units the difference from the linked table was 0.006 on average. The estimator's target is the people the key finds, not every linked person: against the table of the linked people whose certificate shares the key, the estimate on the linked units differed by 0.0010 on average, with 3 of twenty cells outside the model interval and a z-score standard deviation of 1.43 (0.33-1.04 by size class). Against the whole linked table, 13 cells lay outside the model interval (z-score standard deviation 9.14): that departure, about 0.005 on average, is the difference between the people who share the key and the rest, the selection the estimator cannot see (A1), not an error of its interval.
Comparison with simpler estimators. On all infant units below 25,600 births a year the mean absolute difference from the linked table was 0.004 for the estimator, 0.006 for the unique-key link, 0.035 for uniform attribution, 0.039 for the Goodman regression and 0.068 for the candidates without the control (Supplementary Table S7); for admissions 0.006, 0.008, 0.006, 0.009 and 0.016. The unique-key link uses 25% of the infant units below 25,600 births a year, and the estimator's advantage is the larger the more chance candidates a unit has (Supplementary Table S7).
The estimator was not better everywhere (Supplementary Table S7). Among infants in municipalities under 400 births a year, where a key is almost always unique, the unique-key link was closer (0.0039 against 0.0049). In admissions below 6,400 certificates a year, where chance candidates are few, the candidates without the control and the simpler estimators were as close or closer; the control paid off where chance candidates were many, among infants from 400 births a year and in admissions from 6,400 certificates.
By age at death, the admissions' estimate on the linked units matched its target in every band (Supplementary Table S9): <1 0.0042 (0 of 20 cells outside); 1-4 0.0015 (0 of 20 cells outside); 5-14 0.0011 (0 of 20 cells outside); 15-24 0.0016 (0 of 20 cells outside); 25-59 0.0011 (1 of 20 cells outside); 60+ 0.0016 (2 of 20 cells outside). This validates it on hospital deaths of children aged 1-14, though not on deaths outside hospital at those ages. Across infants by size, admissions by size and admissions by age, accuracy depended on the expected number of chance candidates per unit, the scaled control (Figure 2D): below about one per unit the mean difference from the target stayed under 0.007, and above it rose to 0.018 and 0.10. That quantity, which any application can compute before estimating, defines the estimator's domain better than the municipality's size.
Simulation. With the assumptions holding and the real number of deaths (ideal, twins), the largest bias of the five commonest cells was 0.004 below 6,400 births a year and 0.009 at 6,400-25,599 (up to 3.0 Monte Carlo errors). The Indigenous row, rare among the units and common among the chance candidates, was biased by up to 0.082 at 6,400-25,599 (3.7 Monte Carlo errors; Supplementary Table S8, Figure 3, Supplementary Table S1); over the 44 size-by-quantity cells below 25,600 births, 4 (ideal) and 4 (twins) had a bias beyond 1.96 Monte Carlo errors. With a twelfth of the deaths (sparse) the five commonest cells reached 0.020 below 6,400 births and 0.134 above; this is a heavy-tail effect and not a small-sample bias of a ratio of sums: at 6,400-25,599, 14% of replicates had a non-positive row sum, so that the ratio has no finite moments, the median bias of Black recorded Black was -0.021 against a mean of +0.134 (sd 0.819), and failed replicates are counted in the means. Twins raised the share found from 95.9% to 97.0% and left the proportions as they were. In these scenarios the model intervals covered the system's classification in 92%-100% of replicates (the Monte Carlo band of a coverage of 95% is +/-3.0 points), the cluster intervals in 89%-100% and the earlier counts' intervals in 86%-100%, too wide for common cells and too narrow for rare ones; the chance intervals covered the table of the deaths found in 90%-100%. In the pooled classes the model standard error overstated the empirical one (mean model SE over empirical SD 1.36 for Black recorded Black in all municipalities, 0.99 under 400 births a year), and coverage there was 98.5%: conservative, not nominal. Movers certified where they died, 26% of deaths lost from the candidates, biased the proportions against all deaths: White recorded White by +0.013 under 400 births a year, while against the deaths found the bias was -0.0003; coverage of all deaths fell to 86%. Moving unequal by race moved the national White to Brown rate ratio from 0.66 to 0.72 (the nominal ratio of the shares found, 1.11), while equal moving left it at 0.66. Moving that depended on the class recorded biased the proportions by up to 0.038 against all deaths (coverage down to 0%) and by at most 0.013 against those found. With A2 broken (boys 52.5% of White and 50.5% of Black births) the proportions were biased by up to 0.011 and the national White to Brown ratio by +0.065. In municipalities of 25,600 births or more a row sum was at or below zero in 28 of 200 replicates without any departure.
The scenarios added after review. A day's births overdispersed as in SINASC 2018 raised the share found to 96.7% (against 95.9%), because a sex-specific excess of births on a day with a death is not removed by the other sex's control, and biased the proportions by up to 0.005. Wrong birth dates at the rates by class of the admissions left the share found at 85.2% and biased the White to Brown rate ratio by +0.019, and by +0.025 when also more frequent when the class recorded differs from the declared. Twelve pooled sparse cohorts, the real regime, gave a largest bias of 0.009 and model coverage of 93%-98%; a municipality effect on the classification (tau 0.42 on the logit) brought the cluster intervals' coverage to 94%-98% against the model intervals' 92%-98%; with every departure together (application) the proportions were biased by up to 0.013 against all deaths.

Discussion
Main findings. The cross-classification of a variable recorded in two registers was estimated without linking any individual, from the birth date, sex and municipality alone, by subtracting the other sex's records of the same day as a negative control. On the units a linkage paired it agreed with the linked table within 0.004 on average over the cells for infant deaths in municipalities under 25,600 births a year, where the candidates without the control missed by 0.071, and within 0.016 in every cell for admissions that ended in death. A shifted key date found nothing. Against its target, the people the key finds, the intervals were adequate for infants (0 of 20 cells outside the model interval) and for admissions (3 of 20); the admissions' linked table departed from that target by about 0.005, the selection of who shares the key. In simulation, with the assumptions holding and the real number of deaths, bias was at most 0.004 below 6,400 births a year for the five commonest cells and up to 0.082 for the Indigenous row at 6,400-25,599; the model intervals covered 92%-100%, within Monte Carlo error of 95% up to 6,400 births a year and conservative above; families moving biased it against all deaths, by up to 0.038 when moving depended on the class recorded.
What is estimated. The estimator describes the people whose key the two registers share, and A1 does not hold for them: findability fell from 91% for certified White to 81% for Indigenous admissions and depended on the class the hospital recorded. Its bias is therefore the selection of who shares the key, in part a property of the estimator's weighting (it weights each person by the probability of being found) and in part of the key. Where linked data exist it is measurable, as here; a linkage that uses other fields (the day of death, the hospital) reaches people the estimator cannot, and comparing linked and unlinked records is the way to see who they are [4]. Run on all units the infants' table departed from the linked one by 0.004 against 0.004 on the linked units; in the admissions the units whose certificate shared the key differed from the rest by 12.6 percentage points in White recorded White, but 10.0 once the hospital's missing race is set aside: about 2.6 points are extra missing race among the unfindable. The unfindable are therefore partly units with missing race, which suggests records kept less carefully; whether the rest are movers or recording errors was not tested (their age, cause and municipality were not profiled). After infancy, whether the people not found are children whose families moved, or whose birth date or municipality was recorded differently, cannot be told from these data; in the simulation the departure was small when moving was unrelated to the class recorded, because certification differed only through the gradient of classification between places that was put into it, and several times larger when moving followed the class recorded. The simulation shows the mechanism, not its size in the data.
Comparison with linkage methods and ecological inference. Regression on linked data can be corrected when the probability that each pair is a true link is known or modelled [6], and with partially identifying variables unbiased coefficients required removing all wrong pairs [7]. The estimator removes them in expectation instead of one by one: the false agreements are counted where they can be counted, among records that cannot be the person, and the count is subtracted, where linkage estimates its false-match rate from a model of the match weights [5]. Like multiple imputation over all candidate records [9] it uses every candidate, but it needs no model of which candidate is the person; unlike a latent-link likelihood over each death's candidate set it gives a unit with one candidate the weight of a unit with thirty-six, which is why it fails where the key is shared by many births. It is closest in spirit to ecological regression [12,13], whose Goodman form, on the same cells, had 8.7 times the estimator's mean difference for infants below 25,600 births a year. Its price relative to linkage is that it gives tables, not linked records: no individual covariate of one register can be related to the other's beyond the classes counted, though any class of either register can be added to the counting (the certifier, the mother's schooling). The closest published method fits a contingency table by maximum likelihood from incorrectly linked data [22]: it starts from record pairs already formed by a linkage, estimates from a clerically reviewed sample the probability that a link is correct in each cell, and replaces each linked pair's class with its expectation given that probability, iterating by EM; it assumes links are incorrect at random, independent of the outcome given the other file's class, and treats unlinked records as non-response [8,23]. Where the key is shared by several births no pair is formed and no match probability is modelled, so it cannot be run on the same units; the estimator replaces the match model by the other sex's records of the same day as the measure of chance agreement.
Variance. The earlier practice took each cell's counts as Poisson and ignored the row's sum in the denominator of a proportion; its intervals were too wide for common cells and too narrow for rare ones. With the denominator, the shared keys and the control's scale, the model interval was conservative or close to nominal in the simulation up to 6,400 births a year (92%-100% against a Monte Carlo band of +/-3.0 points) and conservative in the pooled classes, where the mean model standard error was 1.36 times the empirical one. These coverages hold where the assumptions hold; where they break, coverage of the people of the whole table fell, to 0.00 at worst when moving depended on the class recorded and 0.60 when key errors depended on it. On the real units, against its target, the z-scores of the cells had a standard deviation of 0.72 (infants) and 1.43 (admissions); against the whole linked admissions table it was 9.14, because the units are so many that the selection of who shares the key, small as it is, dwarfs the sampling error. By size class among infants, the model interval missed 0 of 20 cells at <400, 2 of 20 cells at 400-1,599, 1 of 20 cells at 1,600-6,399, 0 of 20 cells at 6,400-25,599; the limit of 25,600 births a year was chosen on the same data. The shifted-date placebo shows how an empirical null could calibrate the intervals [16,17]: its z-scores had a standard deviation of 0.77-1.15, close to 1, so the interval is right for chance. The chance interval answers another question, the distance from a perfect linkage of the same units. The cluster error treats the municipalities as a sample of places and includes the variation of the classification between them; it exceeded the model error for the admissions, possibly because the classification varies between municipalities, which a simulation without a municipality effect cannot test. Which error to report for a national table is therefore left open until the intervals are calibrated on real data.
Assumptions. A2 requires that the two sexes born on a day share the composition by class and the sex ratio of the municipality and year. For births the sex ratio is nearly constant, and the sexes' day-to-day excess variation is almost wholly shared (in SINASC 2018 each sex's daily births' variance beyond Poisson was 0.0075 of the mean squared, and the part shared by the two sexes 0.0069); in the simulation a two-point difference in the share of boys between White and Black births biased the White to Brown ratio by +0.065. For a register of deaths of all ages it is not exact: in 2019, 28.1% of the certificates of men aged 15-29 gave White against 39.3% of women's, and men's were 3.9 times as many, against 1.01 at 60 or more. In the admissions the chance candidates were few, and scaling within the municipality or the state changed nothing; with many chance candidates in such a register, the control should be scaled within finer strata (age as well as place). A1 fails when moving, or the recording of the birth date and municipality, depends on the class recorded given the class declared. Unequal shares found by declared class leave the proportions unbiased and scale the rate ratios by the ratio of the shares; the data cannot identify them without linked records, but linked data do give them (Results).
Limitations. First, neither validation tests the setting in which the estimator is needed, so it is validated for infant and hospital deaths and not yet for ages 1-14: at 1-12 years 26%-39% of deaths are not found, by moving or by recording in proportions unknown, against 10% of infant deaths; there the simulation stands in. Second, above 25,600 births a year (seven municipalities) the chance candidates swamp the person and the estimates fail; the limit was chosen on the infant validation itself, and results without those municipalities should be given beside national ones. Classes rare among the units and common among the chance candidates (Indigenous deaths where Indigenous births are few) have unstable rows. Third, the simulation's world was built from the real births and the linked infants' classification, but its municipalities are fixed, the movers' destinations, the differential moving and the twins' mortality were set, not measured, and the heterogeneity of classification between municipalities is only the decile gradient, so it cannot tell whether the cluster error is right. Fourth, the known answers are themselves linkages with about 1% of false links. The paper makes no statement about race; that is left to the papers that use the estimator. Fifth, the working limit is not a municipality's size but the expected chance candidates per unit, which the limit of 25,600 births a year only approximates; combining key cells by inverse variance, instead of discarding the largest municipalities, was not attempted. Sixth, the rare Indigenous row is unreliable above 1,600 births a year.
The reference. Two findings of the companion papers weaken the birth record as the reference: it leans toward the local majority, recording mothers whiter than the census in whiter places and less White in less White ones (logit slope 1.13 within regions, 1 if it followed the census), and where hospitals rarely record Black on the admission it counts about half the Black mothers the census holds (5.2% against 10.9%). For the estimator, these concern what the table means, not how well it is estimated: it reproduces the birth record's classification, whatever its departures from self-declaration.
Use. Where two public registers share a date of birth, a sex and a place but no identifier, as Brazilian death certificates after infancy do with birth records, the table between them can be estimated, with its variance, for the people who kept the key, provided the expected chance candidates per unit are few (here, municipalities under 25,600 births a year). It is validated for infants and hospital deaths and not yet for ages 1-14. The functions are released with the data package.
In conclusion, this study set out to state, validate and give the variance of an estimator of the cross-classification of a variable recorded in two registers that share no identifier, only the birth date, sex and municipality of residence. Candidates less the other sex's records of the same day reproduced linked tables between births and deaths and between admissions and certificates on the units that were linked, without linking anyone, and found nothing when the key date was shifted. The estimate is of the people whose key the registers share, with the error of the chance agreements and the denominator, an interval that held for both infants and admissions against that target; its bias is the selection of those people, measurable only where a linkage with other fields exists.
