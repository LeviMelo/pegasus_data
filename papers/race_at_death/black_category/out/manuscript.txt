Black children recorded as Brown: the Black category on the death certificates of children of Black mothers, against the family, the hospital and the certificates of adults, Brazil, 2013-2023
[Authors, affiliations, ORCID, funding, conflicts of interest and contributions: to be supplied by the authors; not part of the blinded text]
Abstract
Objective: To measure how often the Black category is used for children of Black mothers on Brazilian death certificates, set it against how families, hospitals and certificates classify Black people at other moments, and find by whom, where and when it is used.
Methods: Cross-sectional study of linked birth, hospital and death records and of census microdata, Brazil. Infant deaths of 2013-2023 were linked to their birth records (the mother's declaration of her own race) and the birth records to the mothers' delivery admissions (the hospital's record of the mother); the family's classification of children came from the 2010 Census. The certificate's category was related to the hospital's record of the same mother within hospitals, to each delivery hospital's practice by logistic regression with clustered errors, and to the certifier, the child and the place by multilevel logistic regression, in space and over time.
Results: Of infants of mothers who declared themselves Black, 0.18 (95%CI 0.17; 0.20) were certified Black, 0.55 (95%CI 0.53; 0.57) Brown and 0.18 (95%CI 0.16; 0.21) White; counted net, certificates recorded 0.34 as many Black infants as there were infants of Black mothers. Hospitals recorded Black mothers as Black at delivery in 0.36 (95%CI 0.33; 0.39) of admissions with a race (net ratio 0.74). Within the same hospital, an infant whose mother the hospital had recorded as Black had 2.04 (95%CI 1.80; 2.32) times the odds of being certified Black, and the delivery hospital's practice predicted the certificate at equal composition, region and year. The category was used more for children of 1-4 (0.24) and 5-9 years (0.29) than for infants in the same municipalities (0.18), less by verification services and medical examiners, and it rose from 0.15 in 2013 to 0.21 in 2023, faster than in the hospital.
Conclusion: Black families are recorded as Brown more often than as Black in Brazil's health records, and the infant certificate undercounts Black infants by about two thirds even net. The certificate follows the hospital's record of the same mother, and the category's use varies with the institution, the certifier and the place, so statistics of the Black population built from these records undercount it unevenly across the country.
Keywords: Race Factors; Racial Groups; Death Certificates; Hospital Records; Medical Record Linkage; Infant Mortality; Brazil.
Introduction
Brazil classifies its population in five categories of colour or race, and the boundary between two of them, preto (Black) and pardo (Brown), is where classification is least stable. Interviewers and respondents agree least at the darker end of the colour continuum [1]; the same person moves between categories across surveys, in both directions [2]; people choose other terms than the census's according to education, colour and the local population [3]; and skin colour carries inequalities that the census categories do not capture [4,5]. Black and Brown people also differ in the discrimination they report, and Brown people differ among themselves by status [6]. Statistics and policy nonetheless often group Black and Brown as negros, and the verification commissions of affirmative action now decide by observation who belongs to the category [7]. Since 2010, Brazilians, especially those of darker skin, have increasingly identified as Black [8].
Health records add classifications made by others. The Ministry of Health instructs that the race on the death certificate be asked of the informant [9], but in practice the certifier or hospital staff often enter it [10]; the hospital records the race of its patients on the admission; and since 2011 the live birth record carries the mother's declaration of her own race. How the Black category moves between these records matters for statistics: a change in how race was recorded on birth records in 2012 changed infant mortality by race without any change in deaths [11], and infant mortality by race estimated from the records was underestimated, most for Brown children [12]. We know of no study that followed the Black category through the records of the same families: the mother's declaration, the hospital's record of the mother, the certificate of her child.
This study aimed to measure how often the Black category is used for children of Black mothers on Brazilian death certificates, set it against how families, hospitals and certificates classify Black people at other moments, and find by whom, where and when it is used.
Methods
Design. This is a cross-sectional study of linked birth, hospital and death records and of census microdata, reported according to the RECORD statement [13]. Deaths came from the Mortality Information System (SIM), live births from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC) and delivery admissions from the Hospital Information System (SIH), public files of the Ministry of Health [14]; the family's classification from the sample of the 2010 Census [15]. All files carry no names; records were related through the variables they share.
Records of the same families. (1) Infant deaths of 2013-2023 were linked one-to-one to their birth records by probabilistic linkage [16] on birth date, sex, residence, birth weight and mother's age (80.5% linked; estimated false links 0.57-0.75% a year); the birth record gives the mother's declaration of her own race. (2) Each birth record of 2012-2023 was linked to the mother's delivery admission in a hospital of the Unified Health System by the mother's birth date, the hospital and the day of birth within the admission, which gives the race the hospital recorded for the mother herself (0.53-0.61 of births linked a year, about the public system's share of deliveries; estimated false links 0.65-0.92%; an obstetric diagnosis on 96.6-98.8% and the same municipality of residence on 95.7-97.5% of linked admissions); hospitals that recorded over a tenth of their patients as Yellow (Asian) were left out as writing a default (490 hospitals), from all analyses. (3) The family's classification is the race a household reported for a child under 10 against the race it reported for the child's mother, in the 2010 Census sample; it concerns other children than those who died. (4) For adult women, deaths at 15-49 years in hospital in 2015-2022 were related to the birth records on which they were mothers and to the admission that ended in death (exploratory; Supplementary Text S1). After infancy, deaths at 1-9 years were related to the births of the same birth date, sex and municipality, with births of the other sex as a control for chance agreement (Supplementary Text S2).
Measures. For people who declared themselves Black, or whose mothers did: the proportion recorded Black by each record, and the categories used instead. For linked infant deaths of Black mothers, the certificate's category by the race the hospital recorded for the same mother at the delivery. For each hospital with 30 or more Black mothers, the share of them it recorded as Black over 2012-2023, its practice, set against the certificates of Black mothers' infants delivered in it. Gross shares were complemented by net counts: the number each record called Black against the number who declared themselves Black. The primary analyses were the infant certificate against the mother's declaration and against the hospital's record of the same mother; the others are exploratory.
Analysis. Proportions from the linkage carry 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) from a bootstrap over municipalities, those of the hospital from a bootstrap over hospitals. The hospital's record of the mother was related to the certificate within delivery hospitals by a Mantel-Haenszel odds ratio. The use of the Black category on infant certificates was modelled by logistic regression with a random intercept for the municipality [17], with the municipality's racial composition (a natural spline with three degrees of freedom on the logit of the White share), standardised socioeconomic context and health services, the certifier, the place and age at death, the cause, the mother's schooling and age, the birth weight, the region and the year per decade (fitted with nAGQ = 0), and with a crossed random intercept for the establishment of death; spatial structure by Moran's I [18,19] and Besag-York-Mollié models with penalised-complexity priors [20,21]; change over 2013-2023 by binomial mixed models with random intercepts and slopes for 133 intermediate regions, the hospital's change by a quasi-binomial model of yearly counts. The delivery hospital's practice, as the logit of its share (with 0.01 added to both counts' shares), entered a logistic model of the certificate, with errors clustered by municipality, alone and with composition (a spline with three degrees of freedom), region and year; the hospital is dated by the birth, the certificate by the death. Analyses used Python 3.11 and R 4.4; the code accompanies the article. Ethics: public, anonymised data; under Resolution 510/2016 of the National Health Council no review was required.
Results
Of 309,203 infant deaths of 2013-2023 linked to their birth records, 22,305 were of children of mothers who declared themselves Black; for 10,568 of them the birth was linked to the mother's delivery admission in the public system. Across all deliveries of 2012-2023 linked to their birth records, 939,740 admissions of mothers who declared themselves Black carried a race.
Who records a Black person as Black
Every record used the Black category less than the mothers had declared it (Table 1, Figure 1). Hospitals recorded mothers who declared themselves Black as Black at delivery in 0.36 (95%CI 0.33; 0.39) of admissions with a race, as Brown in 0.53 (95%CI 0.49; 0.56) and as White in 0.10 (95%CI 0.09; 0.11) (intervals from a bootstrap over hospitals), and left the race blank in 21.8%; with blanks counted, 0.28 were recorded Black. Death certificates recorded the infants of Black mothers as Black in 0.18 (95%CI 0.17; 0.20), as Brown in 0.55 (95%CI 0.53; 0.57) and as White in 0.18 (95%CI 0.16; 0.21). Families reported as Black 0.39 of the children under one year of Black mothers in 2010.
Gross agreement overstates the loss when a small group is misclassified into a large one and back. Counted net, the hospitals recorded 697,191 mothers as Black against 939,740 who declared themselves Black, a ratio of 0.74, because a small share of the far more numerous Brown mothers was recorded Black. The certificates recorded 7,560 linked infant deaths as Black against 22,305 infants of Black mothers, a ratio of 0.34: only 1.6% of Brown mothers' infants were certified Black, too few to make up the loss.
On certificates the Black category was used more for older children: 0.24 (95%CI 0.20; 0.27) at 1-4 years and 0.29 (95%CI 0.23; 0.36) at 5-9 years, against 0.18 (95%CI 0.17; 0.18) for infants in the same municipalities (those with fewer than 25,600 births a year, where the estimator after infancy works), and, for adult women who died in hospital and had declared themselves Black as mothers, 0.55 (95%CI 0.48; 0.63) (exploratory; Supplementary Text S1, Supplementary Table S1), with the hospital recording them Black in 0.37 (95%CI 0.31; 0.44). Families too reported older children as Black more often (0.47 at 1-4 and 0.52 at 5-9 years).

The hospital and the certificate
When the hospital had recorded the infant's own mother as Black, the infant was certified Black in 0.28 (95%CI 0.27; 0.29) of deaths and Brown in 0.47 (95%CI 0.45; 0.48); when it had recorded her as Brown, Black in 0.12 (95%CI 0.12; 0.13); when as White, Black in 0.17 (95%CI 0.15; 0.20) and White in 0.39 (95%CI 0.36; 0.42) (Figure 2A, Supplementary Table S2). Within the same delivery hospital, the odds of being certified Black were 2.04 (95%CI 1.80; 2.32) times higher when the hospital had recorded the mother as Black (Mantel-Haenszel, 443 hospitals). For mothers who declared themselves Brown, the infant was certified Black in 0.06 (95%CI 0.05; 0.07) when the hospital had recorded the mother as Black and 0.02 (95%CI 0.01; 0.02) when as Brown. Infants of Black mothers were certified Black in 0.19 (95%CI 0.18; 0.19) with a linked delivery admission and 0.18 (95%CI 0.18; 0.19) without one.
Hospitals differed in their practice: across 1,595 hospitals with 30 or more Black mothers, the share recorded Black ranged from 0.01 at the 10th percentile to 0.64 at the 90th (median 0.24; Supplementary Table S3). For 12,988 infant deaths of Black mothers delivered in 1,040 such hospitals (75% of Black mothers' infants with a delivery record died where they were born), the odds of being certified Black were multiplied by 1.36 per unit of the logit of the delivery hospital's share, and by 1.20 (95%CI 1.14; 1.26) at equal municipal composition, region and year; across 328 hospitals with 10 or more such deaths, practice and certificates correlated at 0.47 (0.52 weighted by deaths; Figure 2B).

Who certifies, and the child
In the full multilevel model (Figure 3), death verification services (0.59 (95%CI 0.47; 0.75)) and medical examiners (0.65 (95%CI 0.53; 0.81)) used the Black category less than attending physicians, with the cause and place of death in the model; mothers under 20 had their infants certified Black less often (0.82 (95%CI 0.74; 0.92)), and mothers with 0-3 years of schooling (1.25 (95%CI 1.03; 1.51)) and with 12 or more (1.14 (95%CI 1.02; 1.28)) more often than those with 8-11. The municipality's composition explained 39% of the variance between municipalities, its socioeconomic context 19%, its services 22%, and all terms 58% (Supplementary Table S4); with a crossed random effect for the establishment of death, the variance was 0.31 between establishments and 0.10 between municipalities.

Where and when
The use of the category clustered in space (Moran's I 0.42) and was highest in the South (Figure 4A). Between municipalities, the state explained 37% of the deviance with 26 parameters and the composition 22% with three (24% with the census share of White residents), and residual clustering remained (Moran's I 0.13). From 2013 to 2023 the certificate's use rose by 0.42 (95%CI 0.20; 0.64) in log-odds per decade, from 0.15 to 0.21; the regional slopes ranged from 0.19 in the North to 0.99 in the South per decade, without a clear difference between regions (Figure 4B). The hospitals' recording of Black mothers as Black rose more slowly, by 0.11 (95%CI 0.05; 0.17) in log-odds per decade, from 0.34 for births of 2013 to 0.36 for births of 2023 (Figure 4C), while the share of births to mothers who declared themselves Black rose from 5.6% to 7.9%.

Discussion
Black families were recorded as Brown more often than as Black in Brazil's health records. Counted net, hospitals recorded 0.74 as many Black mothers as declared themselves Black, and death certificates 0.34 as many Black infants as there were infants of Black mothers. The certificate followed the record of the same mother: within the same hospital, the infant of a mother the hospital had recorded as Black was twice as likely to be certified Black. The category was used more for older children than for infants, less by verification services and medical examiners than by attending physicians, and more each year, faster on the certificate than in the hospital.
These results locate the boundary between preto and pardo inside the records that produce health statistics. Studies of classification by observers found that disagreement concentrates at the darker end of the colour continuum [1], that classification moves in both directions over time [2], and that Black and Brown people differ in socioeconomic position and in the discrimination they report [4,6]. Here the observers are hospital staff and certifiers. The net counts separate two things gross agreement mixes: at the hospital, the loss of Black mothers to the Brown category is half made up by Brown mothers recorded as Black, as expected when a small group is misclassified into a large one and back; on the infant certificate it is not, and the Black population is undercounted by two thirds.
Three explanations fit parts of the results. The first is that hospital staff and certifiers perceive the same mother and child, so that appearance leads both to the Black category; it fits the strong association within the same hospital between the hospital's record of a mother and her infant's certificate, which a hospital-wide practice cannot produce. The second is a practice of each institution: hospitals differed widely in how often they recorded Black mothers as Black, and the delivery hospital's practice predicted the certificates at equal composition, although part of this association can be the same perception of the populations each hospital serves. The third is the classification of infants as lighter than older children, by observers and, in the census, by families: the certificate used the category less for infants than for children of 1-9 years in the same municipalities. A certificate completed from the record at hand rather than by asking the informant, against the Ministry's instruction [9], would carry both the hospital's perception and its practice to the certificate.
The rise over 2013-2023 was faster on the certificate than in the hospital, and coincided with more mothers declaring themselves Black on birth records and with a darkening of racial identification in surveys of the period [8]; whether certifiers responded to that change or to training cannot be tested here. A change in how race was recorded on birth records in 2012 changed infant mortality by race [11]; the records that remain filled by staff, the hospital and the certificate, move separately from the mother's declaration and at different speeds.
Strengths and limitations. The study follows the same families through linked records nationally over eleven years, with one-to-one linkage of the infant to the birth and of the birth to the delivery, and separates gross from net classification. Limitations: the mother's declaration on the birth record is the reference, but it is itself entered by staff on the mother's answer; the hospital record covers deliveries in the public system, about half of the infants' mothers, though infants with and without it were certified Black in the same proportion; hospitals that recorded a default Yellow were left out, and a default Brown would go undetected; appearance is not recorded, so a shared perception of the same person cannot be told from a shared practice; the family's classification is from 2010 and concerns other, surviving children; the adult women's comparison is exploratory; the estimates after infancy rest on an estimator that loses precision in the largest municipalities; the multilevel models were fitted by an approximation (nAGQ = 0); and many contrasts are reported beyond the two primary analyses.
Implications. Counts of the Black population from hospital and death records undercount it, and death certificates of infants most, by amounts that differ between hospitals and places; grouping Black and Brown as negros removes that error from comparisons with White people but not from statistics of the Black population itself. Recording who informed the race on the certificate, and auditing hospitals' records against their patients' declarations, which linked records already allow, would show where the category is lost. As committees now decide by observation who is Black in affirmative action [7], these records show how far observation and declaration diverge at the Black boundary.
In conclusion, this study set out to measure how often the Black category is used for children of Black mothers on Brazilian death certificates, set it against how families, hospitals and certificates classify Black people at other moments, and find by whom, where and when it is used. The Black category was used for a minority of the children of Black mothers, least for infants, and on infant certificates the Black population was undercounted by two thirds even net; its use followed the record of the same mother and the hospital, varied with the certifier and the place, and rose over the decade faster on the certificate than in the hospital.
