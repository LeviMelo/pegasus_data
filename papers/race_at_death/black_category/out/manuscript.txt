Infants of Black mothers recorded as Brown: the Black category from the birth record to the delivery admission and the death certificate, Brazil, 2012-2023
[Authors, affiliations, ORCID, funding, conflicts of interest and contributions: to be supplied by the authors; not part of the blinded text]
Abstract
Objective: To measure how often the infants of mothers who declared themselves Black are recorded as Black on Brazilian death certificates, set it against the hospital's record of the same mothers, and test whether the category follows the person, the hospital that records her or the certifier.
Methods: Cross-sectional study of linked birth, hospital and death records and of census microdata, Brazil. Infant deaths of 2013-2023 were linked to their birth records (the mother's declared race) and the birth records to the mothers' delivery admissions (the hospital's record of the mother). The certificate was related to the hospital's record of the same mother within delivery hospitals and within the establishments where infants died elsewhere, to each hospital's practice estimated without the mother, to the certifying physician and to the year, standardised for who the mothers were.
Results: Of infants of mothers who declared themselves Black, 0.18 (95%CI 0.17; 0.20) were certified Black and 0.55 (95%CI 0.53; 0.57) Brown; certificates recorded 0.34 (95%CI 0.32; 0.36) as many infants as Black as there were infants of Black mothers. Hospitals recorded these mothers as Black in 0.36 (95%CI 0.33; 0.39) of admissions with a race. Within the same hospital, an infant whose mother the hospital had recorded as Black had 2.01 (95%CI 1.77; 2.28) times the odds of being certified Black, and 1.75 (95%CI 1.25; 2.44) among infants who died in another establishment, where the delivery chart was not at hand, and 2.53 (95%CI 1.41; 4.54) for mothers with no prenatal visit. One standard deviation of the delivery hospital's practice, estimated without the mother, multiplied the odds by 1.37 at equal composition, region and year, 48% of it through the hospital's record of the mother herself; a Black mother who changed hospital moved by 1.01 of the difference in the two hospitals' practice. Over 2013-2022, the certificate's use of the category rose faster than the hospital's record of the same infants' mothers (difference -0.29 in log-odds per decade, 95%CI -0.50; -0.07).
Conclusion: The infants of Black mothers are recorded as Black on a minority of death certificates, fewer than the hospitals record their mothers. A mother's hospital record follows the level her hospital sets, and the certificate goes with that record also where no chart or shared prenatal record was likely at hand, which is compatible with staff reading the same person alike, or with her own varying answer, and not with copying. Taking the mother's declaration as the reference, these records count fewer infants as Black, by amounts that differ between hospitals.
Keywords: Race Factors; Racial Groups; Death Certificates; Hospital Records; Medical Record Linkage; Infant Mortality; Brazil.
Introduction
Brazil classifies its population in five categories of colour or race, and the boundary between two of them, preto (Black) and pardo (Brown), is where classification is least stable. Interviewers and respondents agree least at the darker end of the colour continuum [1]; the same person moves between categories across surveys, in both directions [2]; people choose other terms than the census's according to education, colour and the local population [3]; and skin colour carries inequalities that the census categories do not capture [4,5]. Black and Brown people also differ in the discrimination they report, and Brown people differ among themselves by status [6]. Statistics and policy nonetheless often group Black and Brown as negros. Since 2010, Brazilians, especially those of darker skin, have increasingly identified as Black [7].
Health records add classifications made by others. The Ministry of Health instructs that the race on the death certificate be asked of the informant [8], but in practice the certifier or hospital staff often enter it [9]; the hospital records the race of its patients on the admission; and since 2011 the live birth record carries the mother's declaration of her own race. How the Black category moves between these records matters for statistics: a change in how race was recorded on birth records in 2012 changed infant mortality by race without any change in deaths [10], and infant mortality by race estimated from the records was underestimated, most for Brown children [11]. We know of no study that followed the Black category through the records of the same families: the mother's declaration, the hospital's record of the mother, the certificate of her child.
This study aimed to measure how often the infants of mothers who declared themselves Black are recorded as Black on Brazilian death certificates, set it against the hospital's record of the same mothers, and test whether the category follows the person, the hospital that records her or the certifier.
Methods
Design. This is a cross-sectional study of linked birth, hospital and death records and of census microdata, reported according to the RECORD statement [12]. Deaths came from the Mortality Information System (SIM), live births from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC) and delivery admissions from the Hospital Information System (SIH), public files of the Ministry of Health [13]; the family's classification from the sample of the 2010 Census [14]. All files carry no names; records were related through the variables they share.
Records of the same families. (1) Infant deaths of 2013-2023 were linked one-to-one to their birth records by probabilistic linkage [15] on birth date, sex, residence, birth weight and mother's age (80.5% linked; estimated false links 0.57-0.75% a year); the birth record gives the mother's declaration of her own race. (2) Each birth record of 2012-2023 was linked to the mother's delivery admission in a hospital of the Unified Health System by the mother's birth date, the hospital and the day of birth within the admission, which gives the race the hospital recorded for the mother herself (0.53-0.61 of births linked a year, about the public system's share of deliveries; estimated false links 0.65-0.92%; an obstetric diagnosis on 96.6-98.8% and the same municipality of residence on 95.7-97.5% of linked admissions); hospitals that recorded over a tenth of their patients as Yellow (Asian) were left out as writing a default (490 hospitals), from all analyses. (3) The family's classification is the race a household reported for a child under 10 against the race it reported for the child's mother, in the 2010 Census sample; it concerns other children than those who died. (4) For adult women, deaths at 15-49 years in hospital in 2015-2022 were related to the birth records on which they were mothers and to the admission that ended in death (exploratory; Supplementary Text S1, Supplementary Table S1). After infancy, deaths at 1-9 years were related to the births of the same birth date, sex and municipality, with births of the other sex as a control for chance agreement (Supplementary Text S2). Measures. For infants of mothers who declared themselves Black, the proportion certified Black, and the categories used instead; for their mothers, the proportion the delivery admission recorded Black. Gross shares were complemented by net counts: the number each record called Black against the number of mothers who declared themselves Black, which counts Brown mothers recorded Black against Black mothers recorded otherwise. The mother's declaration is the reference; it is entered on the birth record by staff on her answer, and its dependence on who filled it (physician, nursing, other staff) was examined within hospitals. The primary analyses were the infant certificate against the mother's declaration and against the hospital's record of the same mother; the others are exploratory.
Analysis. Proportions from the linkage carry 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) from a bootstrap over municipalities, those of the hospital and the net ratios from bootstraps over hospitals and municipalities. Person against place: the hospital's record of the mother was related to the certificate within delivery hospitals (Mantel-Haenszel, with the Breslow-Day test of heterogeneity, and conditional logistic regression with the mother's schooling and age, low birth weight, the year and the infant's age at death); and, to separate copying from the delivery chart, within the establishments of death of infants who died where they were delivered and of those who died elsewhere. The hospital's practice was its share of Black mothers recorded Black over 2012-2023 without the infant's own mother, shrunk by a beta prior fitted to the hospitals, entered as a logit in logistic models of the certificate with errors clustered by hospital, alone, with composition (a spline with three degrees of freedom), region and year, and beside the hospital's record of the mother. The certifier: crossed random intercepts for the certifying physician, the establishment of death and the municipality [16] (Laplace approximation; physicians identified by state and registration, filed in 2014-2018). Time: the yearly proportion standardised to the region, schooling and age of the Black mothers of 2013, and the hospital's record and the certificate of the same infants in one logistic model with a record-by-year interaction and errors clustered by infant. The same mothers across births: births with the same mother's birth date and municipality of residence in two years and a higher parity, in municipalities with fewer than 1,600 births a year, with the mother's birth date moved by one day as a control for chance pairs. Analyses used Python 3.11 and R 4.4; the code accompanies the article. Ethics: public, anonymised data; under Resolution 510/2016 of the National Health Council no review was required.
Results
Of 309,203 infant deaths of 2013-2023 linked to their birth records, 22,305 were of children of mothers who declared themselves Black; for 10,568 of them the birth was linked to the mother's delivery admission in the public system, and these infants were certified Black as often as the others (0.19 (95%CI 0.18; 0.19) against 0.18 (95%CI 0.18; 0.19)). Across all deliveries of 2012-2023 linked to their birth records, 939,740 admissions of mothers who declared themselves Black carried a race.
Three records of the same families
Each later record used the Black category less than the mothers had declared it (Figure 2, Supplementary Table S2). Hospitals recorded mothers who declared themselves Black as Black at delivery in 0.36 (95%CI 0.33; 0.39) of admissions with a race, as Brown in 0.53 (95%CI 0.49; 0.56) and as White in 0.10 (95%CI 0.09; 0.11), and left the race blank in 21.8%; counting the blanks as not Black or as Black, 0.28 to 0.50 of these mothers were recorded Black. Death certificates recorded their infants as Black in 0.18 (95%CI 0.17; 0.20), as Brown in 0.55 (95%CI 0.53; 0.57) and as White in 0.18 (95%CI 0.16; 0.21). Counted net, so that Brown mothers recorded Black offset Black mothers recorded otherwise, the hospitals recorded 0.74 (95%CI 0.68; 0.81) as many mothers as Black as declared themselves Black, and the certificates 0.34 (95%CI 0.32; 0.36) as many infants as Black as there were infants of Black mothers; only 1.6% of Brown mothers' infants were certified Black. In the same municipalities, certificates used the category for 0.18 (95%CI 0.17; 0.18) of infants and 0.24 (95%CI 0.20; 0.27) of children aged 1-4 (the estimate at 5-9 years was too imprecise to compare); families in 2010 reported as Black 0.39 of Black mothers' children under one year.

The reference
The mother's race on the birth record is entered by staff on her answer. Across all linked births, 10.1% of those filled by a physician declared the mother Black, 6.6% of those filled by nursing and 6.8% of those filled by other staff; within the same hospital the differences were -0.7 (standard error 0.4) and -1.0 (0.2) percentage points against nursing: who filled the record hardly changed the declaration, which varied with the hospital's population. Of mothers with two births in the smaller municipalities (2,284,318 pairs; 34.5% expected by chance from the shifted-date control), those who declared themselves Black at the first birth did so again in 0.52 (95%CI 0.52; 0.53), against 0.87 (95%CI 0.87; 0.87) for Brown and 0.75 (95%CI 0.75; 0.76) for White mothers.
Person, chart or hospital
When the hospital had recorded the infant's own mother as Black, the infant was certified Black in 0.28 (95%CI 0.27; 0.29) of deaths; when it had recorded her as Brown, in 0.12 (95%CI 0.12; 0.13); when as White, in 0.17 (95%CI 0.15; 0.20) (Supplementary Table S3). The White cell sat above the Brown one because 42% of these mothers lived in the South, where certificates used the category most, against 3% of those the hospital recorded as Brown; standardised to the regions of all these infants, the three were 0.25, 0.14 and 0.13 (Figure 3A), and the Mantel-Haenszel odds ratio over regions for a Black against any other record was 2.11 (1.90; 2.35). For the same 10,438 infants, the hospital had recorded the mother as Black in 0.37 and the certificate the infant in 0.19. Within the same delivery hospital, at equal schooling and age of the mother, birth weight, year and age at death, the odds were 2.01 (95%CI 1.77; 2.28) times higher when the hospital had recorded the mother as Black (Mantel-Haenszel 2.05, 346 hospitals; Breslow-Day p = 0.003, so the association varied between hospitals). If the certificate copied the delivery chart, the association would vanish where the chart was not at hand. It did not: within the establishments of death, it was 1.98 (95%CI 1.71; 2.29) among infants who died where they were delivered and 1.75 (95%CI 1.25; 2.44) among infants who died elsewhere (838 deaths in 114 establishments with both records informative; Figure 3B). A shared upstream record, such as the prenatal card, would carry one entry to every later record; it is least likely without prenatal care and for deaths at home. Within the delivery hospital, the odds ratio was 2.53 (95%CI 1.41; 4.54) for mothers with no prenatal visit (255 deaths), 2.05 with one to six and 2.26 with seven or more; among the 338 infants who died at home it was 3.37 (1.81; 6.28) over regions. Where the death was investigated, the share certified Black was 0.15-0.22 whatever the source of the investigation, including interviews with the family. For mothers who declared themselves Brown, the infant was certified Black in 0.06 (95%CI 0.05; 0.07) when the hospital had recorded the mother as Black and 0.02 (95%CI 0.01; 0.02) when as Brown.
Hospitals differed in their practice: across 1,595 hospitals with 30 or more Black mothers, the share recorded Black ranged from 0.01 at the 10th percentile to 0.64 at the 90th (median 0.24; Supplementary Table S4, Supplementary Figure S1). With the practice estimated without the infant's own mother and shrunk toward the hospitals' mean, one standard deviation of practice (1.72 on the logit scale) multiplied the odds of being certified Black by 1.63 (95%CI 1.44; 1.85) alone, 1.37 (95%CI 1.22; 1.54) at equal composition, region and year, and 1.18 (95%CI 1.04; 1.33) beside the hospital's record of the mother herself, whose odds ratio was 1.89 (10,054 deaths, 977 hospitals; errors clustered by hospital). Since the hospital's practice shapes how it records the mother, the last estimate is the part of practice that does not pass through her record; 48% of the effect at equal composition passed through it. The same Black mother recorded Black at one delivery was recorded Black again in 0.92 (95%CI 0.91; 0.92) of her deliveries in the same hospital and 0.58 (95%CI 0.56; 0.60) in another, against 0.13 (95%CI 0.12; 0.13) and 0.36 (95%CI 0.34; 0.37) when not recorded Black at the first delivery (odds ratios about 76 in the same hospital, which a hospital copying its own earlier file would also produce, and 2.5 in another); when she changed hospital, her record moved by 1.01 (standard error 0.03, clustered by the second hospital; 1.16 corrected for the 13% of chance pairs) of the difference in the two hospitals' practice (5,678 mothers; Figure 3C): the hospital's level travels with the hospital, not with the mother.

The certifier
Death verification services (0.59 (95%CI 0.47; 0.75)) and medical examiners (0.65 (95%CI 0.53; 0.81)) used the Black category less than attending physicians, with the cause and place of death in the model (Figure 4). Among 9,337 deaths of Black mothers' infants of 2014-2018 with the certifying physician recorded (5,883 physicians), the variance of the log-odds of being certified Black was 0.29 between physicians, 0.42 between establishments and 0.21 between municipalities (Laplace approximation). Mothers under 20 had their infants certified Black less often (0.82 (95%CI 0.74; 0.92)), and mothers with 0-3 years of schooling (1.25 (95%CI 1.03; 1.51)) and with 12 or more (1.14 (95%CI 1.02; 1.28)) more often than those with 8-11.

Over time
The certificate's use of the category rose from 0.15 in 2013 to 0.20 in 2022 and 0.21 in 2023, and standardised to the region, schooling and age of the Black mothers of 2013 from 0.15 to 0.19 in 2022 and 0.23 in 2023: the rise was not explained by the region, schooling and age of the Black mothers, although more mothers declared themselves Black (5.6% of births in 2013, 7.9% in 2023). For the same 10,438 infants, the certificate rose by 0.45 in log-odds per decade and the hospital's record of their mothers by 0.08, a difference of -0.37 (95%CI -0.55; -0.18) (Figure 5B); over 2013-2022 alone, before the hospital's race field changed, the difference was -0.29 (95%CI -0.50; -0.07). Across all deliveries, the hospitals recorded Black mothers as Black in 0.34 of births of 2013 and 0.36 of 2023; admissions of Black-declared mothers with a race rose from 92,004 in 2022 to 119,012 in 2023 because the admission's race, blank for about a fifth of these mothers until 2022, was filled for nearly all from 2023, mostly with categories other than Black, which lowered that year's share. By region, the share certified Black rose in four regions and was highest in the South (Figure 5A).

Discussion
The infants of mothers who declared themselves Black were recorded as Black on a minority of death certificates: for the same 10,438 infants, the hospital had recorded the mother as Black in 0.37 and the certificate the infant in 0.19, and counted net the certificates recorded 0.34 as many infants as Black as there were infants of Black mothers. The certificate went with how the same mother was recorded at delivery, within the delivery hospital and also where the infant died elsewhere, at home or after no prenatal visit. The delivery hospital's practice went with the certificate too, about half of it through how the hospital recorded the mother herself. The category's use rose over the decade, faster on the certificate than in the hospital.
Several readings were set against each other (Table 1). If the certificate copied the delivery chart, the association would vanish where the chart was not at hand; it remained at a similar size among infants who died in another establishment. If a shared upstream record carried the same entry to every later record, such as the prenatal card or the national health card, the association would weaken among mothers with no prenatal visit and among deaths at home; it did not. If a hospital-wide practice decided the category, a mother's record would follow her hospital; it did, by the full difference in practice when she changed hospital, and a mother recorded Black at one delivery was recorded Black again in the same hospital far more often (odds ratio about 76) than in another (about 2.5), which is also what a hospital copying its own earlier file would produce. Beyond the hospital's level, a person component remained: across hospitals, being recorded Black once still raised the odds of being recorded Black again, and within hospitals the mother's record went with the certificate. The data are compatible with staff in different places reading the same person alike at levels each institution sets, as studies of classification by observers describe [1,4]; they are equally compatible with the mother's own varying self-classification [2] reaching the hospital and the informant at death. Appearance and the informant are not recorded, so the shared element cannot be named.
The mother's declaration is itself entered by staff on her answer. Within the same hospital, who filled the record changed the share declared Black by about one percentage point, a small difference against a share of 6.6%; it does not separate the mother's answer from the hospital's way of asking. The declaration is not stable: of mothers who declared themselves Black at one birth, 0.52 did so again at the next, against 0.75 of White and 0.87 of Brown mothers, after the chance pairs were removed. It is the reference here, not a truth. The infant's own race is not observed: the unit of this study is the infant of a Black mother, whose race also depends on the father and is reported by families lighter than the mother's for young children (a companion paper). That a minority of these infants were certified Black measures how the certificate uses the category for the children of Black women, not how many infants were Black.
The rise over 2013-2023 was faster on the certificate than in the hospital also without 2023, the year the hospital's race field changed, and it was not explained by the region, schooling and age of the Black mothers; it coincided with more mothers declaring themselves Black on birth records and with a darkening of racial identification in surveys of the period [7]. Whether certifiers responded to that change or to training cannot be tested here. A change in how race was recorded on birth records in 2012 changed infant mortality by race [10]; the records filled by staff move separately from the mother's declaration and at different speeds.
Strengths and limitations. The study follows the same families through linked records nationally over eleven years, with one-to-one linkage of the infant to the birth and of the birth to the delivery, separates gross from net classification, and sets copying, a shared upstream record, practice and the person against each other. Limitations: the reference is the mother's declaration, entered by staff; the infant's own hospital record was not used; the hospital record covers deliveries in the public system, about half of the infants' mothers, though infants with and without it were certified Black in the same proportion; hospitals that recorded a default Yellow were left out, and a hospital's blanks were bounded rather than imputed; the same mothers across births were identified by birth date and residence in smaller municipalities, with a measured share of chance pairs; the certifying physician is filed only in 2014-2018, with few deaths per physician; the estimate after infancy rests on an estimator that loses precision in the largest municipalities; and the analyses beyond the two primary ones are exploratory.
Implications. Taking the mother's declaration as the reference, hospital and death records count fewer of the Black population as Black, the infant certificate least, by amounts that differ between hospitals; grouping Black and Brown as negros removes that difference from comparisons with White people but not from statistics of the Black population itself. Recording who informed the race on the certificate would show where the category is lost.
In conclusion, this study set out to measure how often the infants of mothers who declared themselves Black are recorded as Black on Brazilian death certificates, set it against the hospital's record of the same mothers, and test whether the category follows the person, the hospital that records her or the certifier. The infants of Black mothers were recorded as Black on a minority of certificates, fewer than the hospitals recorded their mothers; the certificate went with how the same mother was recorded, within levels each hospital sets, also where no chart or shared record was likely at hand; and the category's use rose over the decade faster on the certificate than in the hospital.
