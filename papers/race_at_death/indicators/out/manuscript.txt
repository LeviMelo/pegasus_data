The same deaths, four inequalities: how the classification of deaths and denominators changes racial inequality in infant and child mortality in Brazil, nationally and in 510 regions, 2013-2024
[Authors, affiliations, ORCID, funding, conflicts of interest and contributions: to be supplied by the authors; not part of the blinded text]
Abstract
Objective: To measure how the White to Brown and Black to Brown ratios of infant and child mortality in Brazil change with the classification of the deaths and of the denominators, nationally and in each of 510 immediate regions.
Methods: Cross-sectional study of routinely collected birth and death records, linked where possible. Infant deaths of 2013-2023 were linked one-to-one to their birth records (the mother's race); deaths at 1-9 years were classified by the mother's race through an estimator that relates each death to the births of the same date, sex and municipality, in municipalities with fewer than 25,600 births a year. Rate ratios were computed with deaths and births by the mother's race, with certified deaths over births by the mother's race (the usual infant indicator), with certified deaths over a modelled census-based population, and with deaths and births reclassified as families classify children in the 2010 Census; regional infant ratios came from Poisson models with Besag-York-Mollié effects over 510 immediate regions. The primary contrast was the infant ratio by the mother's race against the usual indicator.
Results: The White to Brown infant mortality rate ratio was 0.81 (95%CI 0.78; 0.84) by the mother's race and 1.31 (95%CI 1.30; 1.32) by certified race over the same births; the Black to Brown ratio was 1.15 (95%CI 1.09; 1.20) and 0.49 (95%CI 0.48; 0.50). At 1-4 years the White to Brown ratios were 0.73 (95%CI 0.67; 0.80) and 1.40 (95%CI 1.37; 1.42). Over the census-based population the ratio depended on the modelled population: 0.80 in infancy and 0.94 at 1-4 years, against 0.79 with the census's own household classification. By posterior means the two infant ratios had opposite directions in 400 of 510 immediate regions; credibly in 115, with the certified ratio credibly above 1 in 446 and the mother's-race ratio credibly below 1 in 159.
Conclusion: Brown children's excess mortality relative to White children, and Black children's relative to Brown, present when deaths and births are classified by the mother, are reversed by the usual infant indicator. Infant and child mortality by race should be computed from linked records or, where that is impossible, read with its denominator named and the composition of the place in mind.
Keywords: Infant Mortality; Child Mortality; Race Factors; Health Status Indicators; Vital Statistics; Medical Record Linkage; Brazil.
Introduction
Racial inequality in child health in Brazil is measured through mortality rates by race [1,2]. The numerator is the race on the death certificate, which the Ministry of Health instructs be asked of the informant and which in practice the certifier or hospital staff often enter [3,4]; the denominator is the race on the live birth record, the mother's declaration of her own race since 2011 [5] and in nearly all records from 2012, or the race households report in the census. When the two disagree, the rate is a ratio of two classifications. Comparisons of infant mortality by race between sources gave differences in opposite directions [1,2], and in the United States the classification of infants at birth and at death altered the rates of smaller groups [6], while certificates misclassify American Indian deaths [7]. A linked cohort of 19.5 million Brazilian births classified by the mother's race found higher mortality before age 5 among children of Brown, Black and Indigenous mothers than among children of White mothers [8].
This study aimed to measure how the White to Brown and Black to Brown ratios of infant and child mortality in Brazil change with the classification of the deaths and of the denominators, nationally and in each of 510 immediate regions.
Methods
Design and data. This is a cross-sectional study of routinely collected birth and death records, linked where possible, reported according to the RECORD statement [9]. Deaths came from the Mortality Information System (SIM) and live births from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC), public files of the Ministry of Health [10], deaths of 2013-2024 and births of 2012-2023; the family's classification from the sample of the 2010 Census [11]; immediate regions from IBGE [12].
The mother's race for each death. Infant deaths of 2013-2023 (383,975) were linked to their birth records by probabilistic linkage [13] on birth date, sex, residence, birth weight and mother's age; 80.5% were linked, with an estimated 0.57-0.75% of false links a year, and the linked share was 81.1%, 81.1% and 80.2% for deaths certified White, Brown and Black (61.9% for Indigenous). Linked deaths with the mother's race were scaled to every infant certificate, which assumes that unlinked deaths are distributed by the mother's race as linked ones; this cannot be checked, since the mother's race of an unlinked death is unknown. Deaths at 1-9 years carry no birth weight or mother's age, and were classified by the mother's race with a candidate estimator: the births of the same birth date, sex and municipality, less the births of the other sex on the same day as a control for chance agreement [14], cross-classified by the race on the birth record and on the certificate (Supplementary Text S1). In infants, where the linkage gives the answer, the estimator reproduced the linkage's proportion of White mothers' infants certified White in municipalities with fewer than 25,600 births a year (0.79 against 0.79 at 1,600-6,399 births) but not above (0.85 against 0.71); after infancy the main estimates are therefore restricted to the smaller municipalities, and all municipalities are a sensitivity analysis.
Rates. Infant mortality: deaths of 2013-2023 over live births of the same years, per 1,000. At 1-4 and 5-9 years: deaths of 2013-2024 of children born in 2012-2023 over the person-years those birth cohorts contribute at each age, per 100,000; the 5-9 year rates therefore cover the cohorts born in 2012-2019. Births with no race or of Yellow (Asian) mothers enter no race's rate.
Four ways of computing a rate. (1) Mother's race: deaths and births (or person-years) by the mother's race. (2) Certified race over births: certified deaths over births by the mother's race, the usual way infant mortality by race is computed from the two systems. (3) Certified race over a census-based population: certified deaths over the population by race of a modelled population account in which census years are census counts and children born after 2010 take their cohort's race shares in the 2022 Census carried back by the state's rate of reclassification (Supplementary Text S2); at 1-4 years it was computed for 2016-2024, when every child of that age was born in 2012 or later. (4) Family's classification: deaths and births by mother's race passed through the proportions in which households classify children by the mother's race at the same age in the 2010 Census.
Regions. For each of the 510 immediate regions, deaths of White and Brown mothers' infants (linked deaths scaled to all deaths of the region) and certified White and Brown infant deaths were modelled as Poisson counts with the births by the mother's race as exposure, a fixed White term, and Besag-York-Mollié (BYM2) effects [15,16] for the region and for the region's White to Brown contrast, on a graph joining regions whose municipalities share a border, with penalised-complexity priors; fitted by integrated nested Laplace approximation (Gaussian approximation, empirical Bayes) [17,18]. Each region's log ratio is the fixed White term plus the region's contrast effect; a ratio is called credible when it has a 95% credible interval (posterior mean plus or minus 1.96 posterior standard deviations) that excludes 1. The ratio of the certified to the mother's-race rate ratio measures how much the certificate changes the inequality relative to the mother's race.
Primary and secondary analyses. The primary contrast is the infant White to Brown ratio by the mother's race against the ratio by certified race over the same births. The Black to Brown ratios, the ratios after infancy, the census-based and family's ratios and the regional ratios are secondary and exploratory.
Uncertainty and sensitivity. National ratios carry the larger of the Poisson standard error and a delete-one-state jackknife; neither includes the error of the linkage or of the population account, and the census-based and family's ratios carry no interval. Sensitivity: deaths with no race on the certificate given the mother's race; municipalities of 25,600 or more births included; the share of the deceased found among the candidates differing by up to 10 points between White and Brown mothers. Ethics: public, anonymised data; under Resolution 510/2016 of the National Health Council no review was required.
Results
Before one year, mortality by the mother's race from the linked deaths was 11.0 per 1,000 live births for children of White mothers, 13.6 for Brown and 15.6 for Black mothers. The White to Brown ratio was 0.81 (95%CI 0.78; 0.84) by the mother's race and 1.31 (95%CI 1.30; 1.32) by certified race over the same births, the primary contrast; the Black to Brown ratio was 1.15 (95%CI 1.09; 1.20) and 0.49 (95%CI 0.48; 0.50), and the Indigenous to Brown ratio 1.60 (95%CI 1.46; 1.75) and 2.48 (95%CI 2.42; 2.54) (Table 1, Figure 1). At 1-4 years the White to Brown ratios were 0.73 (95%CI 0.67; 0.80) by the mother's race and 1.40 (95%CI 1.37; 1.42) by certified race over the same person-years, at 5-9 years 0.72 (95%CI 0.60; 0.85) and 1.19 (95%CI 1.15; 1.23); for Black against Brown at 1-4 years, 1.07 (95%CI 0.91; 1.24) and 0.84 (95%CI 0.80; 0.88). The linked cohort of 2012-2018 births classified by the mother's race [8] agrees: crude White to Brown ratios of 0.79 under one year and 0.69 at 1-4 years.
Over the census-based population, the White to Brown ratio by certified race was 0.80 in infancy, close to the mother's-race ratio, and 0.94 at 1-4 years (2016-2024, against 0.66 (95%CI 0.54; 0.81) by the mother's race in the same years, all municipalities). The census's own household classification, applied by the mother's race, gave 0.88 in infancy, 0.79 at 1-4 and 0.78 at 5-9 years. The census-based value at 1-4 years thus lies between the household classification and no difference, and depends on how the population account carries the 2022 race shares of children born after 2010 back in time (Supplementary Text S2); none of these values carries an interval.

By region the two infant ratios usually pointed in opposite directions, but credibly so in a minority of regions (Figure 2). By posterior means, the infant ratio by certified race had the opposite direction to the ratio by the mother's race in 400 of the 510 immediate regions, holding 82% of births. The certified ratio was credibly above 1 in 446 regions, while the mother's-race ratio was credibly below 1 in only 159: regional White deaths by the mother's race are few, and the model draws each region toward the national term (a White to Brown ratio of 0.90 by the mother's race and 2.13 by certified race). Both were credible in 115 regions, and the certified ratio credibly exceeded the mother's-race ratio in 469. Weighted by births, the certified ratio was 3.15 times the mother's-race ratio where under 10% of births were White, 1.92 at 10-29%, 1.83 at 30-59% and 2.19 at 60% or more: highest where White births are rare. These class means exceed the national ratios because the national ratio pools regions of different composition, whereas each class mean averages regional ratios. The regions where the certificate raised the ratio most are listed in Supplementary Table S1 and every region in Supplementary Data.

The direction of the national ratios did not depend on the choices of the estimator; their size moved within the intervals. With all municipalities, the White to Brown ratio at 1-4 years by the mother's race was 0.66 (95%CI 0.54; 0.81); for that estimate, leaving out one state at a time gave 0.63 to 0.70; and with the share of the deceased found among the candidates differing by up to 10 points between White and Brown mothers it lay between 0.57 and 0.77. By region, the ratios at 1-9 years are in Supplementary Figure S1, and by state in Supplementary Table S2.
Discussion
Over the same deaths, the White to Brown and Black to Brown ratios of infant mortality in Brazil depended on how the deaths were classified. With deaths and births classified by the mother, children of White mothers died less often than children of Brown mothers, and children of Black mothers more often, as in a linked cohort of 19.5 million births [8]. With certified deaths over births by the mother's race, the usual infant indicator, both directions reversed. After infancy the White to Brown ratios behaved in the same way. Over a census-based population the infant ratio was close to the mother's-race ratio; at 1-4 years it was near 1, a value that depends on how the population account dates children's race between censuses and that this study cannot validate.
Why the certificate reverses the ratios over births. The certificate records the mother's race for most children of White mothers but classes a quarter (0.25) of Brown mothers' infants as White and more than half (0.55) of Black mothers' infants as Brown, and some White mothers' infants (0.17) as Brown. Births stay where the mothers declared. Brown births outnumber White births, so the deaths moved from the Brown to the White numerator are many relative to the White deaths they join, and the White rate rises; Black mothers' infants moved into the Brown numerator lower the Black rate. Where White births are rare, the ratio of the two ratios was largest, as this arithmetic predicts; elsewhere the regional differences were smaller and mostly not credible. Earlier Brazilian comparisons of infant mortality by race between sources found differences in opposite directions [1,2]; the present results show how both directions can occur from the same deaths, since the result depends on the denominator and on the composition of the place.
Strengths and limitations. The deaths of infants were linked one-to-one, with a false-link rate under 1% a year and a linked share that differed by less than one point between White, Brown and Black certificates, so the primary contrast does not rest on the estimator used after infancy; the national and regional ratios are computed over the same births. Limitations: the mother's race on the birth record is itself entered by staff and is the reference here, not a truth, so a reversal means a departure from the mother's declaration; unlinked deaths were assumed to fall by the mother's race as linked ones; the estimator after infancy finds only children who did not move and was restricted to municipalities where it reproduces the linkage; the census-based population is modelled between and after censuses and its ratios carry no interval; the family's classification is a 2010 reference applied by mother's race and age, without an interval; the national intervals do not include the error of the linkage; the regional mother's-race ratios rest on few White deaths and are drawn toward the national value; and the analyses beyond the primary contrast are exploratory.
Implications. An indicator of racial inequality in child mortality should classify the deaths as its denominator classifies the births. For infants, linking deaths to their birth records, which the Ministry of Health's own systems allow, gives both by the mother's race. For older children, rates by the mother's race need person-years from births by the mother's race, or a linked cohort. Where certified race must be used, the ratio should be reported with its denominator named and read against the racial composition of the place.
In conclusion, this study set out to measure how the White to Brown and Black to Brown ratios of infant and child mortality in Brazil change with the classification of the deaths and of the denominators, nationally and in each of 510 immediate regions. Brown children's excess infant mortality relative to White children, and Black children's relative to Brown, present with deaths and births classified by the mother, were reversed by the usual infant indicator, nationally and in most regions by posterior means.
