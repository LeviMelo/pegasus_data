Two races in one rate: the child's on the death certificate, the mother's on the birth record, and racial inequality in infant mortality in Brazil, 2013-2023
[Authors, affiliations, ORCID, funding, conflicts of interest and contributions: to be supplied by the authors; not part of the blinded text]
Abstract
Objective: To measure how the White to Brown and Black to Brown ratios of infant and child mortality in Brazil change with the classification of the deaths and of the denominators, nationally and in each of 510 immediate regions.
Methods: Cross-sectional study of routinely collected birth and death records, linked where possible. Infant deaths of 2013-2023 were linked one-to-one to their birth records. Rate ratios were computed with the numerator classified by the mother's race (linked deaths) or by the certificate, and the denominator by the mother's race (births) or by the child's race as households classify infants of mothers of each race (census flows), with delete-one-state jackknife intervals; the change from one to another was decomposed flow by flow, by age at death, cause, year and region.
Results: Infant mortality was 11.0 per 1,000 live births for children of White mothers and 13.6 for children of Brown mothers (White to Brown 0.80 (95%CI 0.77; 0.84); Black to Brown 1.15 (95%CI 1.09; 1.20)). Certified deaths over births by the mother's race, the usual way the rate is computed, gave 1.31 (95%CI 1.17; 1.46) and 0.49 (95%CI 0.42; 0.56); certified deaths over births reclassified as households classify infants gave 0.79 (95%CI 0.58; 1.07) and 0.80 (95%CI 0.66; 0.98). The reversal came almost entirely from Brown mothers' infants certified White, partly offset by White mothers' infants certified Brown, and held in every year, age at death and cause. Counted by the mother's race, 44,851 infant deaths of Brown mothers' children exceeded the White mothers' rate in 2013-2023; by the usual indicator there was a deficit.
Conclusion: The usual infant mortality rate by race divides deaths classified by the child's race by births classified by the mother's, and the mismatch reverses the White to Brown inequality; classified consistently, by the mother or by the child, the children of Brown mothers die more often. Black infants' excess relative to Brown persisted by the mother's race and fell below parity on the certificate even over the child's births.
Keywords: Infant Mortality; Child Mortality; Race Factors; Health Status Indicators; Vital Statistics; Medical Record Linkage; Brazil.
Introduction
Racial inequality in child health in Brazil is measured through mortality rates by race [1,2]. The numerator is the race on the death certificate, which the Ministry of Health instructs be asked of the informant and which in practice the certifier or hospital staff often enter [3,4]; the denominator is the race on the live birth record, the mother's declaration of her own race since 2011 [5] and in nearly all records from 2012, or the race households report in the census. When the two disagree, the rate is a ratio of two classifications. Comparisons of infant mortality by race between sources gave differences in opposite directions [1,2], and in the United States the classification of infants at birth and at death altered the rates of smaller groups [6], while certificates misclassify American Indian deaths [7]. A linked cohort of 19.5 million Brazilian births classified by the mother's race found higher mortality before age 5 among children of Brown, Black and Indigenous mothers than among children of White mothers [8].
This study aimed to measure how the White to Brown and Black to Brown ratios of infant and child mortality in Brazil change with the classification of the deaths and of the denominators, nationally and in each of 510 immediate regions.
Methods
Design and data. This is a cross-sectional study of routinely collected birth and death records, linked where possible, reported according to the RECORD statement [9]. Deaths came from the Mortality Information System (SIM) and live births from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC), public files of the Ministry of Health [10], deaths of 2013-2024 and births of 2012-2023; the family's classification from the sample of the 2010 Census [11]; immediate regions from IBGE [12].
The mother's race for each death. Infant deaths of 2013-2023 (383,975) were linked to their birth records by probabilistic linkage [13] on birth date, sex, residence, birth weight and mother's age (Figure 1); 80.5% were linked, with an estimated 0.57-0.75% of false links a year, and the linked share was 81.1%, 81.1% and 80.2% for deaths certified White, Brown and Black (61.9% for Indigenous). Linked deaths with the mother's race were scaled to every infant certificate, which assumes that unlinked deaths are distributed by the mother's race as linked ones; this cannot be checked, since the mother's race of an unlinked death is unknown. Deaths at 1-9 years carry no birth weight or mother's age, and were classified by the mother's race with a candidate estimator: the births of the same birth date, sex and municipality, less the births of the other sex on the same day as a control for chance agreement [14], cross-classified by the race on the birth record and on the certificate (Supplementary Text S1). In infants, where the linkage gives the answer, the estimator reproduced the linkage's proportion of White mothers' infants certified White in municipalities with fewer than 25,600 births a year (0.79 against 0.79 at 1,600-6,399 births) but not above (0.85 against 0.71); after infancy the main estimates are therefore restricted to the smaller municipalities, and all municipalities are a sensitivity analysis.

SIM: Mortality Information System. SINASC: Live Birth Information System. The census gives the proportions in which households classify infants of mothers of each race, which reclassify births by the child's race (Methods).

Rates. Infant mortality: deaths of 2013-2023 over live births of the same years, per 1,000. At 1-4 and 5-9 years: deaths of 2013-2024 of children born in 2012-2023 over the person-years those birth cohorts contribute at each age, per 100,000; the 5-9 year rates therefore cover the cohorts born in 2012-2019. Births with no race or of Yellow (Asian) mothers enter no race's rate.
Two classifications in each term. The numerator was classified by the mother's race (linked deaths scaled to all certificates by certified race, since the linked share differs by race) or by the race on the certificate, which concerns the child. The denominator was classified by the mother's race (live births of the same years) or by the child's race, obtained by passing the births of each mother's race through the proportions in which households classify children under one year of mothers of that race in the 2010 Census, nationally and by region. The four combinations form a 2 x 2: consistent by the mother, consistent by the child (certified deaths over the child's births), and the two mixed ones, of which certified deaths over births by the mother's race is the usual way infant mortality by race is computed from the two systems. A modelled census-based population and the family's classification applied to deaths and births are reported in the supplement.
Decomposition. Starting from deaths and births by the mother's race, the certificate's flows were switched on one at a time (Brown mothers' infants certified White, White mothers' certified Brown, Black mothers' certified Brown, Brown mothers' certified Black, Indigenous mothers' certified Brown), then the deaths with no race on the certificate, then the denominator by the child's race. The 2 x 2 was repeated by age at death (neonatal 0-27 days, post-neonatal), cause group (perinatal, congenital, infectious or respiratory, external, ill-defined, other), year of death, region, and standardised to the regions (Mantel-Haenszel). Absolute measures: rate differences and the deaths above the White mothers' rate.
Regions. For each of the 510 immediate regions, deaths of White and Brown mothers' infants (linked deaths scaled to all deaths of the region) and certified White and Brown infant deaths were modelled as Poisson counts with the births by the mother's race as exposure, a fixed White term, and Besag-York-Mollié (BYM2) effects [15,16] for the region and for the region's White to Brown contrast, on a graph joining regions whose municipalities share a border, with penalised-complexity priors; fitted by integrated nested Laplace approximation (Gaussian approximation, empirical Bayes) [17,18]. Each region's log ratio is the fixed White term plus the region's contrast effect; a ratio is called credible when it has a 95% credible interval (posterior mean plus or minus 1.96 posterior standard deviations) that excludes 1. The ratio of the certified to the mother's-race rate ratio measures how much the certificate changes the inequality relative to the mother's race.
Analyses. The study is descriptive. The 2 x 2 for infants is the main result; the strata, the ratios after infancy and the regional ratios are exploratory.
Uncertainty and sensitivity. National ratios of the 2 x 2 carry delete-one-state jackknife intervals, which reflect the variation between states but not the error of the linkage or of the population account, and the census-based and family's ratios carry no interval. Sensitivity: deaths with no race on the certificate given the mother's race; municipalities of 25,600 or more births included; the share of the deceased found among the candidates differing by up to 10 points between White and Brown mothers. Ethics: public, anonymised data; under Resolution 510/2016 of the National Health Council no review was required.
Results
Of 383,975 infant deaths of 2013-2023, 80.5% were linked to their birth records; linked deaths were scaled to all certificates by certified race (factors 1.27 for White, 1.29 for Brown, 1.29 for Black and 1.63 for Indigenous certificates), and 8.4% of certificates carried no race.
Two classifications in one rate
By the mother's race in both terms, infant mortality was 10.8 per 1,000 live births for children of White mothers, 13.5 for Brown, 15.4 for Black and 26.4 for Indigenous mothers: White to Brown 0.80 (95%CI 0.77; 0.84), Black to Brown 1.15 (95%CI 1.09; 1.20), Indigenous to Brown 1.96 (95%CI 1.72; 2.23) (Table 1, Figure 2A). With certified deaths over the same births, the usual way, the ratios were 1.31 (95%CI 1.17; 1.46), 0.49 (95%CI 0.42; 0.56) and 2.47 (95%CI 2.08; 2.94). With certified deaths over births classified as households classify infants, they were 0.79 (95%CI 0.58; 1.07), 0.80 (95%CI 0.66; 0.98) and 2.28 (95%CI 1.86; 2.79). Standardised to the regions, the White to Brown ratio was 0.87 by the mother's race, 1.66 the usual way and 0.81 by the child's race; the crude national ratios are pulled toward 1 by the concentration of White births in the regions of lower mortality. The linked cohort of 2012-2018 births classified by the mother's race [8] agrees: a crude White to Brown ratio of 0.79 under one year.

Switching on the certificate's flows one at a time (Figure 2B), Brown mothers' infants certified White moved the White to Brown ratio from 0.80 to 1.61; White mothers' infants certified Brown brought it back to 1.28; the remaining flows and the deaths with no race took it to 1.31; and dividing by births classified as households classify infants returned it to 0.79. For Black to Brown, Black mothers' infants certified Brown took the ratio from 1.15 to 0.57, and the child's denominator raised it only to 0.80: the certificate uses the Black category less than households do, so the Black infants' excess does not return.

Ages, causes, years and absolute measures
The reversal held in every year of 2013-2023 (White to Brown by the mother's race 0.76-0.85, the usual way 1.24-1.39; Figure 2C), in the neonatal period (0.81 and 1.21) and after it (0.77 and 1.56), and for every cause group, from perinatal (0.78 and 1.14) to infectious or respiratory causes (0.57 and 1.18) (Supplementary Table S1). In absolute terms, by the mother's race 44,851 deaths of Brown mothers' infants and 8,543 of Black mothers' infants exceeded the White mothers' rate over 2013-2023 (2.6 and 4.6 per 1,000 live births); the usual indicator showed deficits of 56,205 and 16,418.
After infancy, with deaths related to births through the candidate estimator in municipalities with fewer than 25,600 births a year, the White to Brown ratios were 0.73 (95%CI 0.67; 0.80) by the mother's race and 1.40 (95%CI 1.37; 1.42) by certified race over the same person-years at 1-4 years, and 0.72 (95%CI 0.60; 0.85) and 1.19 (95%CI 1.15; 1.23) at 5-9 years; the shares found among candidates are lower than in infancy and the mother's-race ratios after infancy rest on the assumption that they do not differ by race (by region in Supplementary Figure S1, by state in Supplementary Table S4).
Regions
By region, the usual way reversed the ratio everywhere and the child's denominator did not restore the same value everywhere: certified deaths over the child's births gave a White to Brown ratio of 0.74 in the North, 0.52 in the Northeast, 1.00 in the Southeast, 1.35 in the South, 0.88 in the Centre-West, against 0.96, 0.88, 0.85, 0.84, 0.88 by the mother's race. In 510 immediate regions, modelled separately for each classification with Besag-York-Mollié effects, the certified ratio was credibly above 1 in 446 regions and the mother's-race ratio credibly below 1 in 159; few White deaths per region draw the mother's-race estimates toward the national term (0.90), so its map is mostly the prior (Figure 3, Supplementary Table S2). Weighted by births, the certified ratio was 3.15 times the mother's-race ratio where under 10% of births were White and 1.83 at 30-59%.

Discussion
The usual infant mortality rate by race in Brazil divides deaths classified by the child's race, on the certificate, by births classified by the mother's, on the birth record. Classified consistently, by the mother in both terms or by the child in both, the children of Brown mothers died more often than those of White mothers; the usual rate reversed this. The reversal came from one flow above all, Brown mothers' infants certified White, which households also classify as White in similar proportion: once the births are classified as households classify infants, the certified ratio returned to the mother's. It held in every year, before and after the first month and for every cause, so it is a property of the rate, not of a period or a kind of death. Earlier comparisons of infant mortality by race between sources found differences in opposite directions [1,2], and inconsistent classification of numerator and denominator is a known source of bias [6,7]; the 2 x 2 shows its size and direction for Brazil.
The Black category behaves differently. By the mother's race, Black mothers' infants died more often than Brown mothers'; on the certificate they were mostly classified Brown, and the ratio stayed below 1 even over births classified as households classify infants, because the certificate uses the Black category less than households do (a companion paper examines who records it). For Black infants the usual indicator and the consistent one by the child disagree for a reason other than the mismatch of terms.
Strengths and limitations. Infant deaths were linked one-to-one to their births, with linkage scaled by certified race, and every term of the 2 x 2 comes from the same deaths and births. Limitations: the mother's race is a staff-entered declaration and the reference here, not a truth; the child's race in the denominator comes from households' classification in 2010, applied nationally and by region, without an interval of its own; the jackknife intervals do not include the error of the linkage; unlinked deaths were assumed to fall by the mother's race as linked deaths of the same certified race; the analysis after infancy rests on an estimator whose shares found cannot be checked by race; and the regional models were fitted separately for each classification, so the regional contrasts between them carry no joint uncertainty.
Implications. A rate of infant mortality by race should classify the deaths and the births alike. Linking deaths to their birth records, which the Ministry of Health's own systems allow, gives both by the mother's race; reporting the child's race requires a denominator of children classified the same way. Read as it is usually computed, the rate hides an excess of tens of thousands of deaths among the children of Brown mothers.
In conclusion, this study set out to measure how the White to Brown and Black to Brown ratios of infant and child mortality in Brazil change with the classification of the deaths and of the denominators, nationally and in each of 510 immediate regions. The usual rate mixes the child's race and the mother's, and the mixture reverses the White to Brown inequality in infant mortality; consistently classified, the excess of Brown mothers' children is present, and that of Black mothers' children is present by the mother's race.
