Other findings on the draft text:
- Introduction: "race is recorded by a physician or a registrar". The Ministry manual says otherwise (7b below): the informant is asked, and the physician must not decide from observation. Suggested: "where the race of the deceased is, by the Ministry's instruction, reported by the person who informs on the deceased, and in practice often entered by the certifier or hospital staff [Travassos]".
- Discussion: "a medical examiner for most external deaths" has no reference; CFM Resolution 1.779/2005 (7c) supports it for violent and non-natural deaths (the "most" is the author's claim; the Ministry manual makes an exception for places without a medico-legal institute).

### B1. Classification of children by parents; shifts with status
1. Schwartzman LF. Does money whiten? Intergenerational changes in racial classification in Brazil. Am Sociol Rev 2007;72(6):940-963. doi:10.1177/000312240707200605. CR, AB. VERIFIED. PNAD 2005: highly educated non-white parents more often classify their children as white, because they marry white partners more and, among interracial couples, label children white more often. A 1996 comparison suggests the effect weakened after shifts in racial politics. Most direct precedent for the paper's reading of "declared race of children" by parent status.
2. Marteleto LJ. Educational inequality by race in Brazil, 1982-2007: structural changes and shifts in racial classification. Demography 2012;49(1):337-358. doi:10.1007/s13524-011-0084-6. CR, AB. VERIFIED. PNAD 1982-2007: the Black-Brown schooling gap narrowed; both structural change and shifts in racial classification contribute.
3. Telles E, Paschel T. Who is Black, White, or mixed race? How skin color, status, and nation shape racial classification in Latin America. Am J Sociol 2014;120(3):864-907. doi:10.1086/679252. CR, AB. VERIFIED. 2010 and 2012 survey data: in Brazil status "polarizes" classification rather than whitening it uniformly.
4. Bailey SR, Telles EE. Multiracial versus collective Black categories: examining census classification debates in Brazil. Ethnicities 2006;6(1):74-101. doi:10.1177/1468796806061080. CR, AB. VERIFIED. Education, age, colour, sex and local composition structure the choice among census terms; a popular drift away from official terms with age.
5. Telles EE. Race in Another America: the significance of skin color in Brazil. Princeton University Press, 2004. doi:10.1515/9781400837434 (book; chapter 4, Racial classification). CR for the book record only; content UNVERIFIED (not read).
Not found: a Brazilian study that relates the race of children to the mother's race using linked records. Searches in Crossref returned only Sansone (2003, book chapter "Negro Parents, Black Children", doi:10.1057/9781403982346_2, content UNVERIFIED).

### B3. Quality of race in SIM and SINASC; linked records; infant and child mortality by race
1. dos Santos HG, do Nascimento CF, de Oliveira Duarte YA, Kawachi I. Blurred lines: racial misclassification in death certificates in Brazil. Int J Public Health 2020;65(1):29-36 (Crossref: vol 65, issue 1, 2019 online). doi:10.1007/s00038-019-01321-1. CR, AB (Europe PMC, PMID 31848636). VERIFIED. Sao Paulo, SABE survey self-reported race against the certificate for 1,012 decedents aged 60 or more: 17.3% misclassified (13.1% whitening, 4.2% darkening); misclassification 63.5% for self-reported Brown and 42.6% for Black; corrected rates reverse the official ranking (Black highest). Closest Brazilian counterpart to this paper; must be cited.
2. Hahn RA, Mulinare J, Teutsch SM. Inconsistencies in coding of race and ethnicity between birth and death in US infants. JAMA 1992;267(2):259 (first page, per Crossref). doi:10.1001/jama.1992.03480020069033 (Crossref lists only Hahn as author and page 259; PubMed PMID 1727523 gives the authorship in the title record). CR, AB. VERIFIED. US 1983-1985 linked birth/infant death file: inconsistency 1.2% white, 4.3% Black, 43.2% other races; 87.3% of those reclassified at death became white; consistent definition changes infant mortality rates by up to 47-79% for small groups. The US precedent for linked birth-death comparison.
3. Gava C, Cardoso AM, Basta PC. Infant mortality by color or race from Rondonia, Brazilian Amazon. Rev Saude Publica 2017;51. doi:10.1590/s1518-8787.2017051006411. CR, AB. VERIFIED. Cad Saude Publica 2019;35(12):e00223218. doi:10.1590/0102-311x00223218. CR, AB. VERIFIED. Completeness of race on certificates rose sharply to excellent from 2007, but small and Northeast municipalities stay worse.
5. Pedraza DF. Qualidade do SINASC: analise critica da literatura. Cienc Saude Coletiva 2012;17(10):2729-2737. doi:10.1590/s1413-81232012001000021. CR, AB. VERIFIED. Review of 13 studies; coverage above 90% in most; does not single out race.

### B8. Linkage error and analysis of ambiguously linked data
1. Lahiri P, Larsen MD. Regression analysis with linked data. J Am Stat Assoc 2005;100(469):222-230. doi:10.1198/016214504000001277. CR, AB. VERIFIED. Linkage errors act like measurement error; with known link probabilities an unbiased regression estimator is derived.
2. Kim G, Chambers R. Regression analysis under incomplete linkage. Comput Stat Data Anal 2012;56(9):2756-2770. doi:10.1016/j.csda.2012.02.026. CR (no abstract read; content UNVERIFIED beyond title). Samart K, Chambers R. Linear regression with nested errors using probability-linked data. Aust N Z J Stat 2014;56(1):27-46. doi:10.1111/anzs.12052. CR only. The Chambers 2009 working paper named in the brief was not located.
3. Hof MHP, Zwinderman AH. Methods for analyzing data from probabilistic linkage strategies based on partially identifying variables. Stat Med 2012;31(30):4231-4242. doi:10.1002/sim.5498. CR, AB. VERIFIED. Two estimators correcting linkage error in regression; unbiasedness needs all wrong pairs removed, which demands strong assumptions on the data structure. Directly relevant to partially identifying keys (date, sex, municipality).
4. Goldstein H, Harron K, Wade A. The analysis of record-linked data using multiple imputation with data value priors. Stat Med 2012;31(28):3481-3493. doi:10.1002/sim.5508. CR, AB. VERIFIED. Carries all candidate matches into the analysis by multiple imputation rather than taking the highest-weight match.
5. Harron K, Wade A, Gilbert R, Muller-Pebody B. Evaluating bias due to data linkage error in electronic healthcare records. BMC Med Res Methodol 2014;14:36. doi:10.1186/1471-2288-14-36. CR, AB. VERIFIED. Linking by date of birth, sex and Soundex: highest-weight classification biases infection rates; prior-informed imputation from match probabilities does better.
6. Harron KL, Doidge JC, Knight HE, Gilbert RE, et al. A guide to evaluating linkage quality for the analysis of linked data. Int J Epidemiol 2017;46(5):1699-1710. doi:10.1093/ije/dyx177. CR, AB. VERIFIED. Gold-standard subset, comparison of linked and unlinked, sensitivity to the linkage procedure: the paper's infant validation matches the first approach.
7. Harron K, Dibben C, Boyd J, Hjern A, et al. Challenges in administrative data linkage for research. Big Data Soc 2017;4(2). doi:10.1177/2053951717745678. CR only. Gilbert R, Lafferty R, Hagger-Johnson G, Harron K, et al. GUILD: guidance for information about linking data sets. J Public Health 2018;40(1):191-198 (online 2017). doi:10.1093/pubmed/fdx037. CR only. Blakely T, Salmond C. Probabilistic record linkage and a method to calculate the positive predictive value. Int J Epidemiol 2002;31(6):1246-1252. doi:10.1093/ije/31.6.1246. CR only (content UNVERIFIED beyond title).
8. Lipsitch M, Tchetgen Tchetgen E, Cohen T. Negative controls: a tool for detecting confounding and bias in observational studies. Epidemiology 2010;21(3):383-388. doi:10.1097/ede.0b013e3181d61eeb. CR, AB. VERIFIED. Outcome and exposure negative controls detect noncausal associations; the opposite-sex candidates in the paper's estimator act as an exposure-type control for chance agreement. A "sibling" design was not found as a named method for this use; none is claimed.
Placement: the candidate-set estimator differs from regression correction (Lahiri-Larsen, Hof-Zwinderman), which needs link probabilities, and from imputation (Goldstein et al.), by estimating a cross-tabulation through subtraction of a control; the draft should state that difference. This sentence is my reading, not a finding of any source.
