## Task C first: Chipperfield et al. (2011)

**Correct citation.** Chipperfield JO, Bishop GR, Campbell P. Maximum likelihood estimation for contingency tables and logistic regression with incorrectly linked data. *Survey Methodology* 2011;37(1):13-24. Statistics Canada cat. 12-001-X, article 11444, ID 12-001-X201100111444. URL: https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/en/catalogue/12-001-X201100111444 (PDF: https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/12-001-x/2011001/article/11444-eng.pdf). Open copy: ANU, http://hdl.handle.net/1885/61411. No DOI is listed. **The authors are not Chipperfield, Bycroft and Chambers**: that attribution in the request is wrong. (Bycroft is not an author; Chambers co-authored the 2015 follow-up, Chipperfield and Chambers, J Off Stat 31(3):397-414.)

**Access caveat.** The Statistics Canada site answered every automated client (curl with browser User-Agent, WebFetch, the in-app browser) with a JavaScript bot-validation page (HTTP 403), and the ANU full-text bitstream requires authentication; I did not try to defeat either. I therefore did NOT read the full text. The abstract and bibliographic data come from the ANU repository record; the method description below is triangulated from three papers that describe it (Chipperfield and Chambers 2015, which I read; Hu and Reiter, arXiv 2511.12343; Tsui, Dasylva and Chu, arXiv 1707.05825). Treat points marked (secondary) as unverified against the original and check them once the PDF is opened in a normal browser.

**Summary (what it assumes, estimates, and whether it fits paper 6).**
1. The paper builds a maximum likelihood estimator for cell probabilities of a contingency table and for logistic-regression coefficients when the analysis file was created by probabilistic record linkage and some links are wrong; it is fitted by the EM algorithm (abstract, ANU record).
2. The method works on the linked file itself: it needs record-level linked pairs in which the study variables of the two files sit side by side (secondary: Tsui et al. describe it as an analysis 'using the linked pairs and a sample of such pairs').
3. It needs a model of linkage quality: an estimated probability that a linked pair is a true match, given the covariates and observed response, which is obtained from a clerical-review sample whose true match status is known, assumed error-free (secondary; Chipperfield and Chambers 2015 say the earlier approaches that need a clerical sample have this requirement). It is not driven by raw match weights alone, and it is not a purely unsupervised procedure.
4. The E-step replaces the unobserved correct-link sufficient statistics by their expectations given the linked data and these match probabilities, and the M-step updates the table or regression parameters; adjustments are described for false links and for unlinked records (secondary).
5. What is estimated: the joint (cross-classified) distribution of two categorical variables, one from each file, or a logistic regression of one on the other, corrected for contamination by false links.
6. Applicability to paper 6, where the two registers share only birth date, sex and municipality and no pairs are formed: the estimator as published does not apply, because it takes linked pairs and a match-probability model as input, and here neither exists.
7. What does carry over (my inference, not stated in the paper): the underlying idea that the observed cross-tabulation is a mixture of true-link and false-link counts, and that the false-link component can be modelled from a known mechanism. In paper 6 the false-link (chance candidate) component is estimated directly from the other sex's records of the same day, which plays the role that the clerical sample plays in Chipperfield et al.
8. A natural way to cite it is therefore as the closest published likelihood treatment of 'linked-file contamination', stressing that paper 6's estimator is count-based and needs no pair-level link or clerical sample; it should not be presented as an application of Chipperfield et al.
9. If a version of paper 6 ever forms actual candidate pairs, the Chipperfield and Chambers (2015) bootstrap (which replicates the linkage and needs no clerical sample, but does need record-level files) is the more suitable reference to test against.

**Other methods for cross-file tables (verified entries are in the table below).** Lahiri and Larsen 2005 (linear regression, known link probabilities); Chambers 2009 (exchangeable linkage-error model; verified only as a citation); Kim and Chambers 2012 and Chambers and Kim 2015 (estimating equations, clerical sample); Chambers and Diniz da Silva 2020 (linkage paradata); Chipperfield and Chambers 2015 (bootstrap). All of them still start from formed links. Methods that start from files with no common units are statistical matching (D'Orazio, Di Zio and Scanu 2006), which yields bounds under conditional independence rather than a corrected count; it does not use a shared key like birth date, sex and municipality either. I did not find a published method that estimates a cross-file table from chance-candidate counts on a coarse key; the paper-6 estimator should be described as new unless the methods search is widened (this is a negative result from one session of searching, not proof).

## Task A: how families classify children in the census (paper 4)

- **telles1998** (1998) Telles EE; Lim N. Does it matter who answers the race question? Racial classification and income inequality in Brazil. Demography, vol 35, no 4, pp 465-474. DOI 10.2307/3004014
  - Found: With a 1995 national survey that recorded both self- and interviewer-classification, estimated white-brown income gaps were larger under interviewer classification (26% vs 17% after controls), while black-brown gaps barely changed. Shows that who classifies matters, which bears on classification of a child by a parent versus by an enumerator.
  - Verification: verified: Crossref + abstract read
- **schwartzman2007** (2007) Schwartzman LF. Does money whiten? Intergenerational changes in racial classification in Brazil. American Sociological Review, vol 72, no 6, pp 940-963. DOI 10.1177/000312240707200605
  - Found: Using PNAD 2005, shows parents' classification of their children varies with parental education: more-educated nonwhite parents more often call their children white, both because they marry whites more and because their interracial couples label children white more often. The closest published analogue to the census parent-to-child classification question.
  - Verification: verified: Crossref + abstract read (abstract truncated by Crossref at the 1996 comparison sentence)
- **carvalho2004** (2004) Carvalho JAM; Wood CH; Andrade FCD. Estimating the stability of census-based racial/ethnic classifications: the case of Brazil. Population Studies, vol 58, no 3, pp 331-343. DOI 10.1080/0032472042000272375
  - Found: Proposes a way to estimate between-census category switching from cohort balances and applies it to colour categories in 1950-90. Finds a large deficit in the black category (about 38%) with a matching gain in brown, i.e. movement from preto to pardo between censuses.
  - Verification: verified: Crossref + abstract read (Illinois Experts page)
- **francis2012** (2012) Francis AM; Tannuri-Pianto M. Using Brazil's racial continuum to examine the short-term effects of affirmative action in higher education. Journal of Human Resources, vol 47, no 3, pp 754-784. DOI 10.3368/jhr.47.3.754
  - Found: Studies racial quotas at the University of Brasilia (2004). Quotas raised the share of black students and appear to have induced some people to misstate race, while others, especially the darkest-skinned, came to identify as black.
  - Verification: verified: Crossref + abstract read (via RePEc/IDEAS). Crossref also carries a duplicate DOI 10.1353/jhr.2012.0024. Note: Pre-dates Lei 12.711 (university-level quota, not the national law).
- **francis2013** (2013) Francis AM; Tannuri-Pianto M. Endogenous race in Brazil: affirmative action and the construction of racial identity among young adults. Economic Development and Cultural Change, vol 61, no 4, pp 731-753. DOI 10.1086/670375
  - Found: In university survey data, self-reported race depends on parents' race, family status, gender and the quota policy. Students of mixed-race families identify more with the mother's race than the father's, and men report lighter than women (conditional on skin tone). Directly relevant to mother-vs-father and gender patterns.
  - Verification: verified: Crossref + abstract read (RePEc/EconPapers)
- **francistan2015** (2015) Francis-Tan A; Tannuri-Pianto M. Inside the black box: affirmative action and the social construction of race in Brazil. Ethnic and Racial Studies, vol 38, no 15, pp 2771-2790. DOI 10.1080/01419870.2015.1077602
  - Found: Follow-up of University of Brasilia entrants before and after quotas; finds a persistent shift in racial identification from non-black to black and from lighter to darker categories, consistent with state classification reshaping identity.
  - Verification: verified: Crossref + abstract read via search-engine abstract copy (publisher page returned 403)
- **bailey2018** (2018) Bailey SR; Fialho FM; Loveman M. How states make race: new evidence from Brazil. Sociological Science, vol 5, pp 722-751. DOI 10.15195/v5.a31
  - Found: Compares 1995 and 2008 national surveys, before and after race-targeted affirmative action spread: lay racial self-identification increasingly uses the state's categories and matches government classification. Evidence that the policy changed how people declare.
  - Verification: verified: Crossref + publisher page abstract read
- **freeman2025** (2025) Freeman NC; Telles EE; Goldberg RE. The changing relationship between racial identity and skin color in Brazil. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, vol 122, no 1, pp e2411495121. DOI 10.1073/pnas.2411495121
