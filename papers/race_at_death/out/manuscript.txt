The race on the death certificate as a classification of place: the mother's declaration, the family's classification and the certified race of children and young people across 5,570 Brazilian municipalities, 2013-2024

[Authors, affiliations, ORCID, funding, conflicts of interest and contributions: to be supplied by the authors; not part of the blinded text]

Abstract

Objective: To compare the race recorded on the death certificate of people who died before age 25 in Brazil with the race their mothers declared for themselves and with the race families assign to children, to find where in the country and over which years they differ and what explains it, and to measure what the differences do to mortality rate ratios by race.

Methods: Cross-sectional study of routinely collected birth and death records and of census microdata. Infant deaths of 2013-2023 in the Mortality Information System were linked one-to-one to their birth records; deaths at 1-9 years (2013-2024) and at 15-24 years (people born in 2000-2009, municipalities with fewer than 1,600 births a year) were related to the live births of the same birth date, sex and municipality, with births of the other sex as the control for chance agreement. The race households report for children came from the 2010 Census sample. Across 5,570 municipalities, proportions were smoothed by Besag-York-Mollié (BYM2) models, spatial structure was measured by Moran's I and a test at state borders, and composition, state, socioeconomic context, health services and recording quality were set against one another in binomial and multilevel models; mortality rate ratios were estimated nationally and for 510 immediate regions.

Results: Among 296,512 linked infant deaths, the certificate recorded the mother's race for 0.76 (95%CI 0.75; 0.78) of children of White mothers, 0.64 (95%CI 0.62; 0.66) of Brown and 0.18 (95%CI 0.17; 0.20) of Black mothers. Brown mothers' infants were certified White in 0.09 to 0.76 of deaths across municipalities, with a between-municipality variance (log-odds) 5 times that of the family's classification, spatially clustered (Moran's I 0.77). Racial composition explained 71% of the deviance between municipalities, the state 75%, both 81%, and socioeconomic and service context 42% alone and little in addition; in multilevel models composition explained 87% of the municipal variance and context 60%. The certified race stepped at state borders. The White to Brown infant mortality ratio was 0.79 (95%CI 0.75; 0.84) by the mother's race and 1.31 (95%CI 1.30; 1.32) by certified race over the same births, reversed in 400 of 510 immediate regions by posterior means and credibly in 114; at 1-4 years the ratios were 0.66 (95%CI 0.54; 0.81) and 1.43 (95%CI 1.40; 1.45) (0.94 over the census-based population, 2016-2024). At 15-24 years, people recorded White at birth were certified White in 0.68 (95%CI 0.64; 0.71) of natural deaths and 0.35 (95%CI 0.31; 0.39) of assaults by firearm.

Conclusion: The race on the death certificate is a third classification, distinct from the mother's declaration and the family's, and it is associated with place: with the racial composition of the municipality and with the state, more than with wealth or services. It reverses or compresses mortality inequalities between White and Brown children by an amount that differs between regions. The analyses by place, context and cause were exploratory and ecological.

Keywords: Race Factors; Death Certificates; Medical Record Linkage; Spatial Analysis; Child Mortality; Health Status Disparities; Brazil.

Introduction

Mortality by race in Brazil is computed from two classifications made by different people. The numerator is the race on the death certificate. The Ministry of Health instructs that it be asked of the person who informs on the deceased and never decided by the physician from observation [1]; the system does not record who gave the information [2], and in practice the physician or hospital staff often enter it [3]. Deaths from violent causes are certified by the medico-legal services [4]. The denominator is a declaration: the census respondent's, or, on the live birth record since the form of 2011, the mother's declaration of her own race [5]. Rates computed from the two were shown to depend on the source [6,7,8].

Race is not one variable. Self-identification, classification by an observer and classification by relatives are distinct dimensions that agree only in part [9]. In a Brazilian national survey, interviewers and respondents classified people consistently as White, Brown or Black in 79% of cases, with agreement varying with education and with the racial composition of the place [10]; income differences between groups depended on who classified [11,12], and Brown people were reclassified as White more often in whiter cities [13]. Parents do not simply transmit their race to their children: in household surveys, more educated non-White parents classified their children as White more often, partly because of whom they married [14]. Classification by others also responds to the social position and circumstances of the person classified [15,16,17], and on United States death certificates it varied with the cause of death [18].

What this does to mortality statistics has been measured where a second classification of the same person exists. In the United States, certificates agreed with survey reports for White and Black decedents and disagreed for American Indian decedents, whose death rates were understated [19,20,21], and linked birth and infant death records changed infant mortality rates of the smaller groups [22]. In São Paulo, 17.3% of 1,012 older adults had on the certificate a race other than the one they had reported in a survey, and the correction changed the ranking of mortality [23]. A cohort of 19.5 million Brazilian births linked to deaths, classified by the mother's race, found higher mortality before age 5 among children of Indigenous, Black and Brown mothers than among children of White mothers [24]. No study was found that compared, for the same people and for the country, the race on the death certificate with the race on the birth record, or either with the race families assign to their children, and none asked where in the country the two disagree.

Where matters because both the composition of the population and the services that fill certificates vary across Brazil's municipalities. The share of White births ranges from almost none to almost all between neighbouring regions; certificates are completed by hospital physicians, by medico-legal institutes organised by the states for violent deaths, and, where they exist, by death verification services; and the 27 state health secretariats train and audit the certifiers. Three readings of the certificate make different predictions in space. Under the first, it transmits the mother's race, and the agreement between the two should not depend on place. Under the second, it records what the family would answer about the child, as the Ministry's instruction intends; it should then follow the family's classification wherever families classify children differently from their mothers. Under the third, it reflects a classification made by whoever fills it. If that classification draws on the local population, it should move continuously with the racial composition of the municipality, across state borders as within states; if it is an administrative practice, it should change at state borders and with the services that certify; if it follows social position, it should follow the wealth and schooling of the place and of the family.

This study aimed to compare the race recorded on the death certificate of people who died before age 25 in Brazil with the race their mothers declared for themselves and with the race families assign to children, to find where in the country and over which years they differ and what explains it, and to measure what the differences do to mortality rate ratios by race.

Methods

Design and reporting. This is a cross-sectional study of routinely collected birth and death records and of census microdata, reported according to the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statement [25].

Data sources. Death certificates came from the Mortality Information System (SIM) and live birth records from the Live Birth Information System (SINASC), in the public files of the Ministry of Health [26], read in November 2026: deaths of 2013 to 2024 (those of 2023 and 2024 are subject to revision) and births of 2000 to 2023. The classification made by families came from the person records of the sample of the 2010 Population Census [27] (20,635,472 records representing 190,755,799 people). Municipal boundaries were those of the 2022 mesh [28]; immediate and intermediate geographic regions [29], state capitals and metropolitan regions [30] were taken from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE). All files are public and carry no names; records were related through the variables they share, and no identifier was created or kept.

The three classifications. The categories are the five of the official classification: branca (White), preta (Black), parda (Brown), amarela (Yellow) and indígena (Indigenous); Yellow is not shown separately because of small numbers, and Black and Brown, which Brazilian statistics often group as negros, are kept apart. (1) Mother's declaration: on the birth record, the race field became the mother's self-declared race with the form introduced in 2011 [5]; the dedicated field was filled on 2.5% of births in 2010, 55.0% in 2011 and 92.7% in 2012 (Supplementary Table S1), so cohorts born from 2012 were taken to carry the mother's race. For cohorts born from 2000 to 2010 the field is the colour recorded for the newborn on the previous form, called here the colour recorded at birth. (2) Family's classification: the census asks one resident to report the race of every member of the household, and the sample questionnaire identifies each person's mother when she lives in the household, which gives the race reported for the child and for the mother by the same household (6,252,654 pair records of children under 25; 94.1% of children aged 1-4 and 76.5% of people aged 15-19 lived with their mother). It concerns other children than those who died: it is a population reference, applied by the mother's race, the child's age and the place, and not a second label of the deceased. (3) Certificate: the race field of the death certificate. Figure 1 shows how the records relate.

Linkage of infant deaths. Deaths before one year of age in 2013 to 2023 (383,975 certificates) were linked to birth records by a probabilistic linkage [31] on birth date, sex, municipality of residence of the mother, birth weight and mother's age, keeping one-to-one pairs. 309,203 (80.5%) were linked and 296,512 had the mother's race on the birth record. The linked share was compared across certified race, size of municipality, region, year and age at death [32,33]. Each linked pair carries the certifier, place, establishment and underlying cause of the death, and the mother's schooling and age and the birth weight from the birth record.

Deaths after infancy: estimation without individual linkage. After the first year the certificate has no birth weight or mother's age, and a birth date, a sex and a municipality do not identify one birth. For each death, the candidates are all live births with the same birth date, sex and municipality of residence. If the family did not move, one candidate is the deceased; the others agree by chance. The chance candidates were estimated from the births of the other sex on the same day in the same municipality, scaled by the ratio of births of the two sexes: they share the day and the place and cannot contain the deceased, which makes them a negative control [34]. The difference between candidates and control, summed over a group of deaths and cross-classified by the race on the birth record and the race on the certificate, estimates the number of deaths of the group in each pair of classes, times the proportion of the deceased found among their candidates (Supplementary Text S1). The approach estimates a contingency table and not individual links; it differs from methods that correct a regression for linkage error with known link probabilities [35,36] or that impute over candidate links [37]. It was compared with the one-to-one linkage on the infant deaths, by size of municipality.

Study populations. (a) Infant deaths of 2013-2023, linked. (b) Deaths at 1 to 9 years in 2013-2024 of children born in 2012-2023 (77,693 deaths), classified by the mother's race; deaths at 5-9 years come mostly from 2019 onwards (24,532 of the deaths at 1-9 years were in 2013-2018). (c) Deaths at 15 to 24 years in 2015-2024 of people born in 2000-2009 (170,575 deaths), classified by the colour recorded at birth; age and birth cohort are confounded within this window. (d) Children and young people under 25 living with their mother in the 2010 Census sample. (e) For three readings of the same adult, women who died at 15 to 49 years in hospital in 2015-2022 and had been mothers on a birth record (exploratory, Supplementary Text S2).

Variables of the death and of the birth. Age, sex, municipality of residence, year and underlying cause of death (10th revision of the International Classification of Diseases, ICD-10: natural causes, all codes outside chapters V-Y; transport accidents V01-V99; suicide X60-X84; assault by firearm X93-X95; assault by other means X85-X92 and X96-Y09; legal intervention Y35-Y36; other external causes, including events of undetermined intent Y10-Y34), the certifier (attending or substitute physician, medical examiner of a medico-legal institute, death verification service, other), the place of death, the establishment of death, and the mother's years of schooling and age and the birth weight on the birth record. In the census, the mother's level of instruction and the race of her partner, the partner being the person with whom she heads the household, who is not necessarily the child's father.

Variables of the municipality. Racial composition: the share of White mothers among the municipality's live births of 2012-2023. Socioeconomic context: gross domestic product per capita, mean of 2013-2021 [38]; the proportion of people aged 15 or over who could not read and write, the proportion of people with a household income per head of up to half a minimum wage or none, the urban proportion of the population and the population size, all from the 2010 Census [39]. Health services: physicians per 1,000 inhabitants in the December registers of 2013-2023 [40]; and, from the municipality's own death certificates of 2013-2023, the proportion of natural deaths certified by a death verification service, the proportion of external deaths certified by a medical examiner and the proportion of deaths in hospital. Quality of recording: the proportion of the municipality's death certificates with no race. Place: state capital, metropolitan region, macro-region (North: RO, AC, AM, RR, PA, AP, TO; Northeast: MA, PI, CE, RN, PB, PE, AL, SE, BA; Southeast: MG, ES, RJ, SP; South: PR, SC, RS; Centre-West: MS, MT, GO, DF) and state. The variables, their sources and years are listed in Supplementary Table S10.

Measures. (1) For each class of mother (or of colour recorded at birth), the proportion of deaths certified in each race, and for each class of mother in the census, the proportion of children the family classified in each race. (2) Mortality rates by the mother's race: the deaths of an age group were distributed by the mother's race in the estimated proportions and divided by the person-years of the same cohorts, counted from the live births by mother's race (infants: per 1,000 live births of 2013-2023). Rates by certified race used the certified deaths over the same denominators, so that the two differ only in the classification of the deaths; for infants this is the Ministry's own indicator, certified deaths over live births by race. Rates by certified race were also computed over the population by race of a census-based population account (Supplementary Text S3), which is how rates after infancy are usually computed. The rate ratio under the family's classification was obtained by passing both the deaths and the births, by mother's race, through the census proportions of the same age.

Small-area estimation. For each municipality, the linked infant deaths of each class of mother certified in each race, and the census children of each class of mother classified in each race, were modelled as binomial counts with a logit link and a Besag-York-Mollié random effect [41] in the BYM2 parametrisation [42], which splits the between-municipality variance into a spatially structured part (on the queen contiguity of the 2022 mesh, 16,503 pairs of neighbours; 2 municipalities without a land neighbour joined to the nearest) and an unstructured part, with penalised-complexity priors [43]. Models were fitted by integrated nested Laplace approximation [44] (R-INLA, Gaussian approximation, empirical Bayes integration over the hyperparameters); maps show posterior means, and the raw proportions and the widths of the credible intervals are in the supplement. The same model, with poisson counts of deaths of White and Brown mothers' infants over their births and a second BYM2 effect on the White indicator, gave the White to Brown infant mortality rate ratio for each of the 510 immediate regions, by the mother's race, by certified race and through the family's classification of the intermediate region; the ratio of the certified to the mother's-race rate ratio measures how much the certificate misstates the inequality in each region.

Spatial structure and competing explanations. Spatial autocorrelation was measured by Moran's I [45] of the empirical-Bayes standardised proportion [46] and local clusters by the local Moran statistic [47], both with 999 permutations, over municipalities with at least five linked deaths in the class. To set the explanations against one another, binomial models of the municipal proportions were fitted with the composition (a cubic B-spline of the logit share of White births), with state fixed effects, with the municipal context, and with their combinations; for each, the deviance explained, a quasi-likelihood F test of each block given the others, and Moran's I of the Pearson residuals are reported. The border test asks whether the certified race jumps at state borders, as a state practice would make it, or changes continuously, as local composition would [48]: state effects were estimated, with the composition, from the municipalities that touch no other state, and for each pair of neighbouring municipalities in different states the difference of their empirical logits was regressed on the difference of their states' effects and of their composition (slope 1: the full state difference appears at the border; slope 0: none of it does).

Multilevel models. For the linked infant deaths of each class of mother, the certified race (for example, certified White for children of Brown mothers) was modelled by logistic regression with a random intercept for the municipality [49]. A sequence of fixed parts (none; composition; socioeconomic context and place; services and recording; their combinations; and these with the individual variables of the death and the birth, the macro-region and the year) gave the proportional change in the municipal variance and the median odds ratio [50]; the order of entry is reported both ways so that no explanation is credited with variance it shares with another. Models were fitted by penalised iteratively reweighted least squares (nAGQ = 0); for two outcomes the full model was refitted with a crossed random intercept for the establishment of death, to set the place against the institution that certifies.

Time by place. The yearly proportions of linked infant deaths certified with the mother's race (2013-2023) in each of the 133 intermediate regions were modelled by binomial mixed models with a random intercept and a random slope for the year by region; the regional slopes give the change over the decade, and a model with the macro-region by year interaction tests where the change was faster.

Statistical analysis. Proportions from the one-to-one linkage carry 95% confidence intervals (95%CI) from 400 bootstrap resamples of municipalities, because pairs cluster by place; binomial intervals, 3 to 10 times narrower, are in the supplement. For the candidate estimator, the variance of a difference of counts was taken as the sum of the two counts (Poisson). For national rate ratios the standard error was the larger of that and a delete-one-state jackknife. Because the estimator lost precision in the municipalities with 25,600 or more live births per year (Results), proportions for ages 1-9 exclude them and proportions for ages 15-24, where candidates per death are more numerous, are restricted to municipalities with fewer than 1,600 births per year; rate ratios use all municipalities and are repeated without the largest. Proportions by cause of death at 15-24 years were also standardised directly to the distribution of the natural deaths over five classes of municipal composition. An estimate of the candidate estimator is reported as not estimable, in tables and figures alike, when its standard error exceeds 0.15 or it lies outside 0 to 1 by more than two standard errors. The White to Brown ratio at 1-4 years was recomputed with the proportion of the deceased found among the candidates differing by 10 points between White and Brown mothers. Municipalities were grouped by thirds of GDP per capita, weighted by linked deaths, and as state capitals, other members of metropolitan regions and the interior. The linked cohort of Rebouças et al. was compared through the White to Brown ratio of its crude rates. Census proportions are weighted; their standard errors treat the sample as simple and are understated. Local Moran statistics are reported at p < 0.05 without correction for multiplicity. No hypothesis test was pre-registered, and the analyses by composition, place, context, certifier, cause and schooling are exploratory. Analyses used Python 3.11 (DuckDB, statsmodels, esda [51], geopandas) and R 4.4 (R-INLA [52], lme4); the code that produces every number, table and figure accompanies the article.

Ethics. The study used only public, anonymised data and, under Resolution 510/2016 of the National Health Council, did not require review by a research ethics committee.

Results

The one-to-one linkage found the birth record of 80.5% of infant deaths; the linked share was 81.1%, 81.1% and 80.2% for deaths certified White, Brown and Black, 61.9% for Indigenous, and was lower after the first month (64.9%) and in the North (76.2%) than in the South (89.0%) (Supplementary Table S11). Of the 383,971 infant certificates, 2,148 had no sex and 317 no municipality of residence and could not enter the candidate estimator. In municipalities with fewer than 25,600 births per year, the candidate estimator reproduced the linkage's proportions; over all sizes, 0.78 against 0.76 for White, 0.64 against 0.64 for Brown and 0.18 against 0.18 for Black mothers; in the largest municipalities its standard error for White reached 0.12 (Supplementary Figure S1, Supplementary Table S2). The deceased was among the candidates for 71.7% of deaths at 1-9 years and 51.7% of deaths from natural causes at 15-24 years, against 89.8% of infant deaths; the remainder had moved municipality or had no birth record. Three classifications

Families did not classify children as their mothers (Table 2, Figure 2). Before one year, the household reported as White 0.88 of the children of White mothers, as Brown 0.71 of the children of Brown mothers and as Black 0.39 of the children of Black mothers; 0.28 of the children of Brown mothers were reported White and 0.41 of the children of Black mothers Brown. The certificate agreed with the family on the exchange between Brown and White, recording White for 0.25 (95%CI 0.23; 0.27) of the infants of Brown mothers, and departed from it elsewhere: it recorded White for 0.76 (95%CI 0.75; 0.78) of the infants of White mothers, Brown for 0.64 (95%CI 0.62; 0.66) of those of Brown mothers and Black for 0.18 (95%CI 0.17; 0.20) of those of Black mothers, of whom 0.55 (95%CI 0.53; 0.57) were certified Brown. For children of Indigenous mothers the certificate recorded Indigenous in 0.84 (95%CI 0.83; 0.85) of infant deaths, against 0.85 in the family's classification. The certificate left the race blank or recorded another category for 8.8% of the infants of Brown mothers, 8.5% of Black and 5.6% of White mothers.

The municipality

The linked infant deaths gave a median of 8 deaths of Brown mothers' children per municipality (29.3% of municipalities had 20 or more) and of 0 of Black mothers' children (50.3% of municipalities had none). The municipal resolution is therefore supported for the children of Brown mothers, with credible intervals of median width 0.22; other cells, the Black category above all, rest on few deaths per municipality and are shown as smoothed fields (Supplementary Figures S8, S9; the spatial models' parameters in Supplementary Table S13).

The proportion of Brown mothers' infants certified White ranged, across the municipalities in which 90% of births occur, from 0.09 to 0.76 (Figure 3A); the proportion of Brown mothers' children aged 0-9 whom families reported White ranged from 0.10 to 0.36 (Figure 3B). On the logit scale the smoothed municipal values of the certificate varied 4.9 times as much as the family's (variances 1.38 and 0.28; the family's values rest on more children per municipality and are less shrunk, so the ratio is not inflated by smoothing). The variation was spatially structured: the BYM2 mixing parameter was estimated at 0.99, Moran's I of the standardised proportions was 0.77 (p ≤ 0.001 by 999 permutations, 3,459 municipalities), and local statistics at p < 0.05, without correction for multiplicity, marked 546 municipalities of high values among high values in the South, Southeast and Centre-West and 920 of low values in the North and Northeast (Figure 3D). The certificate classed Brown mothers' infants White more often than families class Brown mothers' children where White births were the majority and less often where they were few (Figure 3C, Figure 4A), the two crossing where about 35% of births were White; weighted by births, the certificate exceeded the family by 0.37 in the South and fell short of it by 0.05 in the Northeast. Certified race differed from the mother's in proportions that were also spatially clustered (Moran's I 0.45; Supplementary Figure S10), as was the use of the Black category for Black mothers' infants (Moran's I 0.42; Supplementary Figure S9).

Composition, state and context

For Brown mothers' infants certified White, the municipality's composition alone explained 71% of the deviance between municipalities, the state alone 75%, and the socioeconomic, service and recording context alone 42%; composition and state together explained 81%, and the context added 1% (Table 3, Figure 4B). Composition (3 degrees of freedom) and state (26) each explained about three quarters, and each added to the other (F = 325 for composition given state; F = 66.8 for state given composition; these tests assume independent residuals, which residual clustering contradicts, so their p-values are too small). Part of the deviance is binomial noise (dispersion 8.4 without any term), so the explainable share is below 100%. Moran's I of the residuals fell from 0.71 to 0.29 with composition and to 0.15 with composition and state; in the spatial model, the variance of the municipal field fell from 1.38 to 0.41 and 0.09. Because the composition is measured from the mothers' declarations on the same birth records that give the reference, it was replaced by the share of White residents in the 2010 Census (correlation 0.94): it explained 69% alone and 80% with the state. For Black mothers' infants certified Black, composition explained less (22%) than the state (37%), and residual clustering remained (0.13 with every term).

At state borders, the difference between two neighbouring municipalities in different states was 0.46 (95%CI 0.26; 0.65, errors clustered by the 49 pairs of states) times the difference between their states' effects estimated from municipalities touching no other state (1,069 pairs; Figure 4C); for the certified race differing from the mother's it was 0.35 (95%CI 0.19; 0.52), and for the Black category the state effects estimated away from borders were too unstable for the test. The certified race changes in a step at state borders, as a state practice would make it, and the step is smaller than the difference between the states' interiors; because the estimated state effects carry error, which pulls the slope toward 0, the slope is a lower bound of the step. By place within the state, Brown mothers' infants were certified White in 0.25 of deaths in state capitals, 0.30 in the rest of the metropolitan regions and 0.22 in the interior (Figure 4D); where 30-59% of births were White, the proportions were 0.38, 0.47 and 0.38. At equal composition, municipal wealth changed the proportion less than composition did: where under 30% of births were White, it was 0.15 in the poorest third of municipalities by GDP per capita and 0.23 in the richest; where 60% or more were White, 0.64 and 0.66 (Figure 4E). Intervals are not given for these proportions: binomial intervals understate the clustering of deaths by place.

In the multilevel models of the 178,656 linked infant deaths of Brown mothers' children, the variance between municipalities of the log-odds of being certified White was 1.32 (median odds ratio 2.99). Composition alone explained 87% of it; the socioeconomic context and place alone 55%; the services and recording alone 43%; and composition with both 89%: most of what wealth and services explain alone, they share with composition. The variables of the death and the birth with the macro-region and the year, without any municipal variable, explained 74%: part of what differs between municipalities is who dies there and of what, and where. With everything, 91% was explained and the median odds ratio between municipalities fell to 1.38 (Table 3, Figure 5, Supplementary Table S12). The models were fitted by penalised quasi-likelihood steps (nAGQ = 0), which can understate variance components with binary outcomes; the proportions of variance explained are approximate. For Black mothers' children certified Black, composition explained 39%, the socioeconomic context 19%, the services 22%, the individual variables 48%, and all together 58%.

Residual clustering was better captured by the establishment where the death occurred than by the municipality. With a crossed random effect for the establishment (7,429 levels; deaths outside any registered establishment share one level per municipality), the variance of the log-odds of Brown mothers' infants being certified White was 0.29 between establishments and 0.03 between municipalities, and for Black mothers' infants certified Black 0.31 and 0.10; the Akaike information criterion fell by 3,411 and 266. Where a municipality has one hospital the two levels cannot be told apart, and the composition of a hospital's catchment is not measured, so this does not partition the variance between them. Within the full model (Figure 5B), death verification services certified Brown mothers' infants White more often than attending physicians (odds ratio 1.58 (95%CI 1.49; 1.68)), and both verification services and medical examiners certified Black mothers' infants Black less often (0.59 (95%CI 0.47; 0.75) and 0.65 (95%CI 0.53; 0.81)). Brown mothers' infants were certified White more often when the mother had 12 or more years of schooling (1.73 (95%CI 1.67; 1.80) against 8-11 years) and less often with 0-3 years (0.72 (95%CI 0.67; 0.77)), more often when the death occurred after the first month (1.60 (95%CI 1.54; 1.65) against the first week) and less often when the birth weight was under 2,500 g (0.69 (95%CI 0.67; 0.70)).

Time and place

From 2013 to 2023 the Black category for Black mothers' infants rose by 0.42 (95%CI 0.20; 0.64) in log-odds per decade, nationally from 0.15 to 0.21 (Figure 6A; by region, state, age and year in Supplementary Table S3); the Brown category for Brown mothers' infants rose by 0.35 and the White category for Brown mothers' infants fell by 0.19. The rise was not uniform: the regional slopes for the Black category varied with a standard deviation of 0.47 (likelihood-ratio p = 0.0004), from 0.19 per decade in the North to 0.99 in the South (Figure 6B, 6C), and in 2023 the certificate still used the category for a minority of Black mothers' infants. The overall agreement with the mother's race changed by 0.06 (95%CI -0.00; 0.12) per decade, an interval that includes no change; the negative correlation of regional level and slope (-0.31) is expected in part from regression to the mean. By state, mean agreement over the years was highest in Santa Catarina (0.82) and Rio Grande do Sul (0.77) and lowest in Goiás (0.50) and Espírito Santo (0.54) (Figure 6D).

Mortality under each classification

Table 4 and Figure 7 give mortality by the mother's race and by certified race over the same births. Before one year, mortality was 10.7 per 1,000 live births for children of White mothers and 13.5 for children of Brown mothers (ratio 0.79 (95%CI 0.75; 0.84)); by certified race over the same births, which is the Ministry's indicator, the ratio was 1.31 (95%CI 1.30; 1.32), and over the census-based population under one year 0.80. At 1-4 years the White to Brown ratio was 0.66 (95%CI 0.54; 0.81) by the mother's race and 1.43 (95%CI 1.40; 1.45) by certified race over births; over the census-based population of 2016-2024, the ratio by certified race was 0.94, against 0.66 (95%CI 0.54; 0.81) by the mother's race in the same years. Certification reversed the direction of the ratio when the denominator was declared by the mother and compressed it toward 1 when the denominator was classified as the census classifies. At 5-9 years the ratios were 0.67 (95%CI 0.48; 0.93) and 1.21 (95%CI 1.18; 1.25). Passing deaths and births through the family's classification gave 0.79 at 1-4 and 0.78 at 5-9 years (no interval). For Black against Brown the ratios at 1-4 years were 1.11 (95%CI 0.83; 1.48) by the mother's race and 0.81 (95%CI 0.78; 0.85) by certified race, and for Indigenous against Brown 4.61 (95%CI 3.58; 5.92) and 6.00 (95%CI 5.77; 6.25). Without the municipalities of 25,600 or more births per year the White to Brown ratio by the mother's race was 0.73 (95%CI 0.67; 0.80) at 1-4 years; leaving out one state at a time, it ranged from 0.63 to 0.70; and if the deceased were found among the candidates 10 points less often for White than for Brown mothers, or the reverse, it would be 0.77 or 0.58. A linked cohort of births of 2012-2018 classified by the mother's race [24] reported crude rates that give White to Brown ratios of 0.79 under one year and 0.69 at 1-4 years.

Where the certificate misstates the inequality

In 400 of the 510 immediate regions, holding 82% of births, the White to Brown infant mortality rate ratio by certified race had the opposite direction to the ratio by the mother's race; the ratio by the mother's race was below 1 with a credible interval excluding 1 in 157 regions, the certified ratio above 1 in 446, and both in 114 (Figure 8A, 8B). The misstatement, the ratio of the certified to the mother's-race rate ratio, was largest where White births were fewest (a mean, weighted by births, of 3.15 in regions with under 10% of White births, against 1.83 at 30-59% and 2.19 at 60% or more; Figure 8C, 8D): where White births are rare, a small share of Brown mothers' infants certified White inflates a small White rate. The relation was U-shaped, so that composition and GDP per capita, which were correlated (0.65), each explained little of it linearly (R² 0.06). Of the 25 regions where the certificate most overstated the ratio, 22 were in the North and Northeast (Supplementary Table S14; all regions in Supplementary Data 2).

Ages 15 to 24: cause of death, place and certifier

In municipalities with fewer than 1,600 births per year, people recorded as White at birth who died at 15-24 years were certified White less often as the cause of death moved from natural causes to assault (Table 5, Figure 9): 0.68 (95%CI 0.64; 0.71) for natural causes, 0.62 (95%CI 0.55; 0.69) for suicide, 0.58 (95%CI 0.54; 0.61) for transport accidents, 0.55 (95%CI 0.48; 0.62) for other external causes, 0.47 (95%CI 0.39; 0.55) for assault, other means, 0.35 (95%CI 0.31; 0.39) for assault by firearm, 0.32 (95%CI 0.15; 0.49) for legal intervention. The proportion certified Brown rose from 0.29 (95%CI 0.26; 0.33) to 0.61 (95%CI 0.56; 0.67). People recorded as Brown at birth were certified Brown in 0.65 (95%CI 0.61; 0.69) of natural deaths and 0.83 (95%CI 0.79; 0.87) of assaults by firearm. The difference between natural causes and assault was present in every region (North: 0.31 and 0.19; Northeast: 0.44 and 0.15; Southeast: 0.70 and 0.49; South: 0.86 and 0.65; Centre-West: 0.59 and 0.29) and in both periods, and by single year of age it exceeded twice its standard error at 8 of the 10 ages from 15 to 24 (15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 23) (Supplementary Figure S6); in larger municipalities it could not be estimated.

Assaults were concentrated in municipalities with few White births, where people recorded White at birth were less often certified White whatever the cause (Figure 9B). In the municipalities with under 20% of White births the proportion certified White was 0.39 (95%CI 0.33; 0.45) for natural causes and 0.17 (95%CI 0.13; 0.20) for assaults; with 80% or more it was 0.91 (95%CI 0.81; 1.01) and 0.79 (95%CI 0.65; 0.93). Standardised to the composition of the natural deaths, the proportions were 0.65 (95%CI 0.62; 0.69) for natural causes, 0.55 (95%CI 0.52; 0.58) for external causes other than assault and 0.45 (95%CI 0.40; 0.49) for assaults and legal interventions: the difference between causes was smaller than the crude one, and its point estimate was positive in every class of composition, with overlapping intervals in the classes at the two ends.

A medical examiner certified 9.1% of the natural deaths, 83.1% of the external deaths other than assault and 90.5% of the assaults. Within natural deaths, the proportion of people recorded White at birth who were certified White was 0.69 (95%CI 0.65; 0.74) when a physician certified and 0.58 (95%CI 0.44; 0.73) when a medical examiner did; among deaths certified by a medical examiner it was 0.37 (95%CI 0.34; 0.41) for assaults and 0.60 (95%CI 0.57; 0.63) for other external causes, against 0.43 (95%CI 0.34; 0.51) when a physician certified those. The difference between causes was present within one kind of certifier; between certifiers, within one kind of cause, the intervals were 15 to 30 points wide for natural causes and assaults and cannot exclude differences of 10 points, and for external causes other than assault medical examiners certified White more often than physicians. By place of death, assaults gave 0.45 (95%CI 0.36; 0.54) in a health facility and 0.35 (95%CI 0.30; 0.40) on the street (Supplementary Table S5).

After infancy, and the family's own classification

The dependence on composition held after infancy, with the candidate estimator. At 1-9 years, Brown mothers' children were certified White in 0.23 (95%CI 0.16; 0.29) of deaths in municipalities with under 30% of White births and middle GDP per capita, and in 0.67 (95%CI 0.54; 0.80) where 60% or more were White, with smaller differences by GDP within each class; at 15-24 years, among people recorded White at birth who died of natural causes, the proportion certified White was 0.40 (95%CI 0.34; 0.45) in the poorest municipalities with under 30% of White births and 0.85 (95%CI 0.77; 0.93) in middle-income municipalities with 60% or more, and among assaults 0.16 (95%CI 0.13; 0.19) and 0.66 (95%CI 0.57; 0.76) (Supplementary Table S15). Within the least White class, wealth mattered at 15-24 years: assaults gave 0.16 (95%CI 0.13; 0.19) in the poorest third of municipalities and 0.34 (95%CI 0.20; 0.49) in the richest. Regional rate ratios after infancy are in Supplementary Figure S2 and, by state, in Supplementary Table S9; the composition gradient at 1-9 years is in Supplementary Figure S5 and Supplementary Table S4.

The family's classification itself depended on both parents: children under 10 of Brown mothers were reported White in 0.53 of households where the mother's partner was White, 0.11 where the partner was Brown and 0.09 where the partner was Black (a partner was identified for 73.8% of children), and by the mother's instruction in 0.18 to 0.28 (Supplementary Figure S4, Supplementary Table S6). A model in which the certificate mixes the family's classification with the local distribution fitted the children of White and Brown mothers and not those of Black mothers (Supplementary Figure S3, Supplementary Table S16). Birth cohorts born from 1940 to 2009 moved away from the White category between censuses in 26 of 27 intervals, a description that does not separate reclassification from differential mortality, migration and coverage (Supplementary Text S3, Supplementary Figure S7, Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

The race on the death certificate of a child or young person in Brazil was neither the race the mother had declared for herself nor the race families assign to their children, and it depended on where the child lived. Across municipalities the certificate's classification of Brown mothers' infants as White varied 5 times as much as the family's classification of the same mothers' children, in two large contiguous blocks, whiter than the family in the South, Southeast and Centre-West and less White in the North and Northeast. The racial composition of the municipality's births and the state each accounted for about three quarters of that variation and together for four fifths, with a step at state borders; wealth, schooling, urbanisation, the supply of physicians and the services that certify accounted for less than composition and little in addition to it, and part of the variation between municipalities was who died there and of what. The Black category, used for fewer than one in five infants of Black mothers, became more common over the decade, unevenly between regions. Computed over the same births, the White to Brown mortality rate ratio of children was below 1 by the mother's race and above 1 by certified race, and the misstatement was largest, in a U-shaped relation, where White births were fewest.

The three readings set out in the Introduction make different predictions in space, and the data adjudicate between them. The certificate does not transmit the mother's race: its agreement with her varied with the municipality from one region of the country to another. Nor does it record what the family would answer, as the Ministry of Health instructs [1]. Nationally the two agree on the exchange between Brown and White in infants (0.25 on the certificate, 0.28 in the family), but this agreement is an average of two opposite departures, in the regions where the certificate is whiter than the family and in those where it is less White, and the certificate used the Black category less than half as often as families (0.18 against 0.39). Families themselves classify children by both parents and by schooling [14], but their classification moved with the local composition far less than the certificate did. The data are consistent with the third reading, a classification made by whoever fills the certificate, and its spatial form specifies it: a classification associated with the local population, so that the same child is more often White where most people are White, and with a state component, which could reflect the training, the forms or the practice of each state's certifiers; municipal associations do not say who classifies. The test at state borders, the residual Moran's I after composition and state, and the multilevel variances all point the same way; the alternative that the gradient reflects the wealth or the services of the place was tested and explained less than composition and little in addition to it. What composition left was better captured by the establishments where children died than by municipalities, and the kind of certifier, the mother's schooling and the child's age moved the classification: the classification is associated with the place and, within it, with the institution and the circumstances in which the certificate is filled. Composition was measured from the same birth records that give the reference, but the census share of White residents reproduced its explanatory power.

A classification that follows the local majority is what studies of classification by observers in Brazil describe. Interviewers and respondents agreed more or less according to the local racial composition [10], and self-declared Brown people were reclassified as White more often in cities with more White residents [13]. The certificate shows this at the scale of the country and as a continuous surface: the classification of the same kind of child moved from 0.13 to 0.79 between the least and the most White classes of municipalities, rising with composition within states and stepping at their borders. One part of the certificate does not follow the local majority: the Black category. Its use clustered in space, depended on the state more than on composition, and rose over the decade, most in the South; whether this follows the growth of Black identification seen between censuses [53,54] cannot be tested here. Certificates recorded the declared race of 0.55 (95%CI 0.48; 0.63) of adult Black women who had declared it as mothers (exploratory, Supplementary Table S8), three times the proportion for the infants of Black mothers, in a different population and setting.

At 15 to 24 years, in the municipalities small enough for the estimator, the certified race of people recorded as White at birth depended on the cause of death: about two thirds were certified White when the death was natural and about one third when it was an assault by firearm, with suicide, transport accidents and other external causes in between. Part of this is place: assaults occur where few births are White, and there everyone recorded White at birth is less often certified White. At equal municipal composition the proportions were 0.65 and 0.45. On United States certificates, homicide raised the odds of being certified Black relative to the report of the next of kin [18], and people who had been incarcerated were more often seen as Black by interviewers [55]; classification by others follows cues of social position [15,17]. Three mechanisms can produce the gradient, and the design separates them only in part. The kind of certifier does not account for it: the difference between causes was present among deaths certified by medical examiners, although certifiers may differ by 10 points or more within a cause. The second is the classification of the victim by cues of the death itself, by the certifier or by the informant, who cannot be told apart. The third is selection: the colour recorded at birth is not the person's later classification, and those recorded White at birth who died by assault may be those who came to be seen, or to see themselves, as Brown. For people recorded White at birth in these municipalities, the race in the numerator of a homicide rate depends on the homicide itself, which a declared denominator does not share. The same proportion rose with the schooling the mother had when the person was born, within each of three classes of composition at 15-24 years (not consistently at 1-9 years), as both a classification by social cues and selection would produce.

The reversal of the White to Brown ratio in childhood does not come from the estimator: infants, whose deaths were linked one-to-one, show it (0.79 by the mother's race, 1.31 by certified race over the same births), and the ratio by the mother's race agrees with the crude rates of a linked cohort of 19.5 million births classified the same way (0.79 under one year, 0.69 at 1-4 years) [24]. What the reversal is relative to matters. Certified deaths over live births by race is the Ministry's infant indicator, and it reverses the ratio. After infancy, rates are computed over a census-based population, which classifies children as households do; over that denominator the certified ratio at 1-4 years was 0.94, compressed toward 1 rather than reversed. Under one year, the same denominator brought it to 0.80, close to the mother's-race ratio. Earlier Brazilian comparisons of infant mortality by race between sources found differences in opposite directions [7,8]; in the United States the classification of infants at birth and at death altered the rates of the smaller groups [22], and certificates understate American Indian mortality [21].

Strengths and limitations. The study covers every municipality of the country over eleven to twelve years; it compares the certificate with the mother's declaration for the same child, by one-to-one linkage in infancy, and with the family's classification as a population reference; it measures the spatial and temporal structure of the difference with small-area models and sets the competing explanations against one another rather than adjusting for them. Its limitations are these. (1) The family's classification concerns other children, in 2010, living with their mother; it is a reference by mother's race, age and place, and the rise of Black identification since 2010 would, if anything, widen its gap with the certificate. (2) After infancy the estimator validated in infants (in municipalities with fewer than 25,600 births a year) finds the deceased only if the family did not move (71.7% at 1-9 years, about half at 15-24), assumes that this does not differ by the mother's race (a difference of 10 points would move the 1-4 year ratio within the range printed in the Results), and cannot describe state capitals; the results at 15-24 years rest on municipalities with fewer than 1,600 births a year, a bound chosen after inspecting the estimates. (3) The mother's race on the birth record is itself filled by hospital or registry staff in practice, and may carry a place-dependent component; part of what is attributed here to the certificate could belong to the birth record. 7.3% of 2012 births were still on the earlier form. (4) The composition of the municipality is measured from the same mothers' declarations, over 2012-2023, and is applied to people born in 2000-2009 at 15-24 years; the census share of White residents gave the same results for infants. (5) The explanations were compared ecologically between municipalities and through municipal random effects; a composition effect at the municipal level does not say which person, certifier or informant classifies, and the certifier and the informant cannot be separated because the certificate does not record who gave the race; the deaths' own characteristics explained much of the municipal variance, and the multilevel models were fitted by an approximation (nAGQ = 0) that can understate variance components. (6) Municipal estimates for the Black category are pooled through the spatial field and rest on few deaths; the time analysis uses intermediate regions, and the mortality ratios immediate regions, because municipal counts by year and by race do not support them. (7) Standard errors of the candidate estimator treat counts as Poisson; census standard errors ignore the sample design; the rate ratio under the family's classification has no interval. (8) The analyses by place, context, certifier, cause and schooling were not pre-specified, and many contrasts are reported. (9) Deaths of 2023 and 2024 may be revised.

Implications. For statistics of mortality in childhood, a rate ratio by race depends on who classified the deaths and the denominators and, now, on where. Infant and child deaths that can be linked to their birth records can be classified by the mother's race, as linked cohorts already do [24], which removes the dependence on place; where that is not possible, indicators by certified race should be read against the racial composition of the place, because the same child is more often certified White where White people are the majority, and the misstatement of the inequality is largest where White births are fewest (Supplementary Data 2 lists every immediate region). Recording on the certificate who informed the race, as the instructions presuppose, would allow the classification to be audited, and the state differences found at borders suggest that state-level training is one place to act. For deaths from violence at young ages, comparisons by certified race combine the classification with the cause of death; the grouping of Black and Brown used in Brazilian statistics on violence [56,57] removes the exchanges between those two categories but not those between White and Brown, which were the largest at 15-24 years.

In conclusion, this study set out to compare the race recorded on the death certificate of people who died before age 25 in Brazil with the race their mothers declared for themselves and with the race families assign to children, to find where in the country and over which years they differ and what explains it, and to measure what the differences do to mortality rate ratios by race. The certificate agreed with neither the mother nor the family. It followed the racial composition of the municipality and the state, with a step at state borders, and at 15-24 years the cause of death; it used the Black category for 0.18 of the infants of Black mothers, more often each year and unevenly in space; and with births as the denominator it reversed the direction of the White to Brown mortality rate ratio in childhood, by posterior means in 400 of 510 immediate regions and credibly in 114, with the largest misstatement where White births are rare. The design does not establish which classification is correct; it establishes that the certificate is a third one, made under conditions of place that the other two do not share.
